# Aspirin for Cardiovascular Disease Prevention: A Drug Repurposing Story

## The Story in Brief

In 1897, chemists at Bayer synthesized acetylsalicylic acid—aspirin—as a pain reliever and fever reducer. For decades, it served exactly that purpose: a workhorse medication for headaches, arthritis, and fevers. But in the 1940s, a suburban California doctor named Lawrence Craven noticed something peculiar. His tonsillectomy patients who chewed aspirin gum bled excessively—so much that many needed hospitalization. Understanding that blood clots caused heart attacks, Craven had an audacious idea: if aspirin prevented clotting enough to cause bleeding, perhaps it could prevent the deadly clots that trigger heart attacks.

From 1948 to 1956, Craven prescribed daily aspirin to over 8,000 male patients and observed virtually no heart attacks among them. He published his findings in obscure medical journals, and the medical establishment largely ignored him. Tragically, Craven himself died of a heart attack in 1957—off aspirin because he'd fallen outside his own recommended age range of 45-65. It would take another 17 years before the first randomized controlled trial in 1974, led by Peter Elwood in Cardiff, Wales, showed that aspirin reduced mortality after heart attack. The landmark ISIS-2 trial in 1988 finally proved beyond doubt that aspirin saved lives during acute heart attacks, preventing 25 deaths per 1,000 patients treated. In 1985, the FDA approved aspirin for secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease—nearly four decades after Craven's first observations.

## Why It Matters

Aspirin's journey from pain reliever to cardiovascular guardian represents one of medicine's most impactful drug repurposing discoveries. Today, aspirin is the most widely used medication globally, with approximately 50-120 billion pills consumed annually. The cardiovascular application alone has saved millions of lives. The ISIS-2 trial demonstrated that aspirin reduced vascular mortality by 23% when given during acute myocardial infarction—a finding that transformed emergency cardiac care worldwide.

The impact extends beyond individual lives. Aspirin costs pennies per dose, making it accessible even in low-income countries where cardiovascular disease mortality is rising fastest. Yet despite its proven efficacy, only 40% of people with established cardiovascular disease worldwide take aspirin for secondary prevention—dropping to just 16.6% in low-income countries. This massive treatment gap represents millions of preventable deaths annually.

## Key Facts

- **Original use**: Pain relief, fever reduction, anti-inflammatory (approved 1899)
- **Repurposed use**: Cardiovascular disease prevention (FDA approval 1985 for secondary prevention)
- **The pivotal discovery**: Lawrence Craven, 1948—observed that aspirin-induced bleeding in tonsillectomy patients suggested antiplatelet effects that could prevent heart attacks
- **The mechanism**: Sir John Vane discovered in 1971 that aspirin irreversibly inhibits cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), blocking thromboxane A2 production and preventing platelet aggregation
- **Time gap**: 51 years from synthesis (1897) to Craven's observations (1948); 88 years to FDA approval for cardiovascular use (1985)
- **Impact metrics**:
  - Global market: $2.5-3.4 billion annually (2024)
  - 50-120 billion pills consumed per year worldwide
  - ISIS-2 trial (1988): 25% reduction in vascular mortality during acute MI
  - Physicians' Health Study (1989): 44% reduction in first heart attack risk
  - Estimated millions of lives saved since widespread adoption

## The Lesson

Aspirin's story teaches us that existing drugs can harbor hidden therapeutic powers waiting to be discovered through careful clinical observation. Lawrence Craven's insight came not from laboratory experiments but from paying attention to an unexpected side effect in his surgical patients. His tragedy—dying of the very condition he'd identified aspirin could prevent—underscores how slowly medical breakthroughs can propagate, even when the evidence is compelling. It took four decades of clinical trials, meta-analyses, and scientific debate before aspirin's cardiovascular benefits became standard care. The lesson for drug repurposing today: observe carefully, publish rigorously, and persist through skepticism. The next aspirin may already be sitting in medicine cabinets worldwide.
