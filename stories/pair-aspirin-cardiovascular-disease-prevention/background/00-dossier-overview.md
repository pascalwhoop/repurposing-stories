# Dossier Overview: Aspirin for Cardiovascular Disease Prevention

## Executive Summary

**Drug**: Acetylsalicylic acid (Aspirin)
**Original Indication**: Analgesic, antipyretic, anti-inflammatory agent
**Repurposed Indication**: Cardiovascular disease prevention (primary and secondary)
**Discovery Timeline**: 1897 (synthesis) → 1948 (Craven's observations) → 1985 (FDA approval for secondary prevention)

## The Arc

Aspirin represents perhaps the most clinically significant drug repurposing discovery in modern medicine. Synthesized in 1897 by chemists at Bayer as an improvement over harsh salicylates, aspirin dominated the analgesic market for half a century before anyone suspected its cardiovascular potential. The pivot came from an unexpected source: Lawrence Craven, a general practitioner in Glendale, California, who noticed excessive bleeding in patients chewing aspirin-containing gum after tonsillectomy. Between 1948-1956, Craven prescribed daily aspirin to over 8,000 patients and reported zero heart attacks—findings published in obscure journals and promptly ignored by mainstream medicine.

Craven died of a heart attack in 1957, never seeing his hypothesis validated. It took until 1971 for John Vane to elucidate aspirin's mechanism (COX-1 inhibition), earning him a Nobel Prize in 1982. The first randomized controlled trial came in 1974 (Peter Elwood, Cardiff), showing inconclusive but promising mortality reduction. The breakthrough arrived with ISIS-2 in 1988: 17,187 patients, 25% reduction in vascular mortality, evidence so compelling it transformed global cardiac care overnight. The Physicians' Health Study (1989) confirmed a 44% reduction in first heart attacks, cementing aspirin's role in primary prevention.

## Clinical Significance

- **Mortality Impact**: ISIS-2 showed 23% reduction in 5-week vascular mortality during acute MI; translates to 25 deaths prevented per 1,000 patients treated
- **Secondary Prevention**: Reduces risk of recurrent MI and stroke by ~20-25%
- **Primary Prevention**: 44% reduction in first MI (Physicians' Health Study, 1989) in apparently healthy men over 50
- **Global Health**: 50-120 billion pills consumed annually; most widely used pharmaceutical worldwide

## The Controversy

The repurposing narrative has two layers of controversy:

1. **The Discovery Credit**: The traditional account credits Felix Hoffmann (1897) with synthesizing aspirin, motivated by his father's arthritis. However, Arthur Eichengrün, Hoffmann's supervisor and a Jew, claimed in 1949 that he directed the research and that Nazi revisionism erased his contributions. Modern historical analysis supports Eichengrün's claim.

2. **The Delayed Recognition**: Lawrence Craven's observations (1948-1956) were published but ignored for decades. Reasons included: publication in obscure journals (Mississippi Valley Medical Journal), lack of placebo controls, and contemporary skepticism that such a common drug could prevent heart attacks. The medical community demanded rigorous randomized trials, which took 26 years to materialize (1974) and 40 years to become definitive (1988).

## Research Gaps & Modern Context

Despite overwhelming evidence for secondary prevention, aspirin use remains suboptimal globally: only 40.3% of people with established CVD take aspirin (16.6% in low-income countries, 65% in high-income countries). Primary prevention recommendations have evolved: modern guidelines (2019-2022) now recommend against routine aspirin for primary prevention due to bleeding risks, except in select high-risk individuals. The risk-benefit calculus shifted as statin therapy and blood pressure control improved.

## The Takeaway

Aspirin's repurposing from analgesic to cardiovascular drug saved millions of lives but took 88 years from synthesis to regulatory approval. The delay stemmed from: (1) a single physician's observations being dismissed by the establishment, (2) the long lag between clinical observation and mechanistic understanding, and (3) the requirement for large-scale RCTs that took decades to organize and fund. This case study exemplifies both the enormous potential of drug repurposing and the structural barriers—publication bias, funding gaps, and institutional skepticism—that can delay life-saving discoveries for generations.
