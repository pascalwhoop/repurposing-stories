# The Origin: The Intended Use

## The Synthesis: August 10, 1897

On August 10, 1897, in the laboratories of Friedrich Bayer & Co. in Elberfeld, Germany, a chemist successfully synthesized a stable, pure form of acetylsalicylic acid[^1]. The traditional story credits Felix Hoffmann (1868-1946), a 29-year-old chemist who supposedly wanted to help his father suffering from rheumatoid arthritis. However, historical evidence suggests the real architect was Arthur Eichengrün (1867-1949), Hoffmann's supervisor, whose Jewish identity led to his erasure from the official narrative during the Nazi era[^2].

## The Chemistry: Acetylation Strategy

**The Problem**: Salicylic acid (isolated from willow bark in the 1820s-1830s) was the only effective treatment for rheumatic pain and fever, but it caused severe gastric distress, nausea, and tinnitus. Patients struggled to tolerate the high doses required (5-10 grams daily)[^3].

**The Solution**: Bayer's pharmaceutical research group, headed by Arthur Eichengrün, pursued a systematic strategy of "acetylating" active compounds—adding an acetyl group (CH3CO) to improve tolerability or efficacy[^4].

This approach had already succeeded twice:
1. **Phenacetin** (1888): Acetylated p-nitrophenol → effective fever reducer
2. **Tannin** (1894): Acetylated tannic acid → antidiarrheal medication

**The Synthesis Process**:
Hoffmann combined salicylic acid with acetic anhydride through a reflux reaction, producing acetylsalicylic acid (ASA)[^5]:

```
Salicylic acid + Acetic anhydride → Acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin) + Acetic acid
```

The critical achievement was not the synthesis itself—French chemist Charles Frédéric Gerhardt had first synthesized ASA in 1853—but creating a chemically stable, pure form suitable for mass production and medical use[^6].

## The Controversy: Who Deserves Credit?

### The Official Story (1934-1949)

The first written account appeared in a 1934 history of chemical engineering by Albrecht Schmidt, published shortly after the Nazi Party took power in Germany. This account credits Hoffmann exclusively and introduced the sentimental narrative about his father's arthritis[^7].

Key claims:
- Hoffmann independently consulted chemical literature
- He synthesized ASA to help his rheumatic father
- He worked autonomously, motivated by filial devotion

### The Counter-Narrative: Eichengrün's Account

Arthur Eichengrün contested this story in two crucial documents:

1. **1944 Letter from Theresienstadt**: While imprisoned in the concentration camp at age 76, Eichengrün typed a letter to Bayer claiming he had directed the aspirin research[^8]

2. **1949 Publication**: Shortly before his death in December 1949, Eichengrün published "Fifty Years of Aspirin" in *Pharmazie*, stating[^9]:
   - He instructed Hoffmann to synthesize acetylsalicylic acid
   - Hoffmann merely followed directions without understanding the purpose
   - Eichengrün tested the compound on himself first
   - He arranged clandestine clinical trials through Dr. Felix Goldmann when head pharmacologist Heinrich Dreser rejected the drug

### Modern Historical Consensus

Historian Walter Sneader's research (2000) in the BMJ strongly supports Eichengrün's account[^10]:

**Evidence for Eichengrün**:
- He was head of Bayer's pharmaceutical research group; Hoffmann was a junior chemist
- Eichengrün held 47 patents for other important innovations (including cellulose acetate)
- After aspirin's success, Eichengrün was promoted to head all pharmaceutical and photographic research (1901)
- Hoffmann was transferred to sales—an unusual move for a supposed inventor
- Dreser's laboratory notebooks show no testing of ASA in 1897; testing began only in 1898 after Eichengrün intervened[^11]
- The 1934 account emerged exactly when Nazi racial laws stripped Jews of recognition

**Eichengrün's silence until 1944**: He likely feared jeopardizing his position during the Nazi rise to power. By 1944, imprisoned in Theresienstadt, he had nothing left to lose.

**Most historians now credit Eichengrün as the primary architect**, with Hoffmann as the competent chemist who executed the synthesis.

## The Mechanism of Action (Original Understanding)

In 1897, scientists had no understanding of aspirin's mechanism. The goal was purely empirical: create a salicylate derivative that worked without causing gastric distress[^12].

**What they knew**:
- Salicylic acid reduced pain, fever, and joint inflammation
- The carboxylic acid group (COOH) likely caused stomach irritation
- Modifying this group might preserve therapeutic effects while reducing side effects

**What they didn't know**:
- Prostaglandins didn't exist (not discovered until the 1930s)
- Cyclooxygenase enzymes were unknown
- The concept of platelet aggregation was decades away
- Any connection to cardiovascular disease was unimaginable

## Initial Testing and Approval

### Dreser's Rejection and Eichengrün's End-Run

Heinrich Dreser, Bayer's chief pharmacologist (appointed 1897), initially rejected acetylsalicylic acid, claiming it had an "enfeebling" effect on the heart and declaring "The product has no value"[^13]. At the time, Dreser was focused on promoting another Hoffmann synthesis: diacetylmorphine, which Bayer would market as "Heroin" (from the German *heroisch*, meaning heroic)[^14].

Eichengrün, frustrated by Dreser's rejection, bypassed him by going directly to Felix Goldmann, Bayer's Berlin representative, to arrange unofficial clinical trials with cooperating physicians[^15].

### Clinical Results (1898)

The covert trials showed remarkable results:
- Effective pain relief comparable to salicylates
- Significantly reduced gastric distress
- Well-tolerated at therapeutic doses
- Effective for rheumatic fever, arthritis, and general pain

These positive results convinced Bayer management to overrule Dreser and proceed with commercialization.

### Market Launch (1899)

On March 6, 1899, Bayer patented the name "Aspirin"[^16]:
- **"A"** = acetyl group
- **"spir"** = *Spiraea ulmaria* (meadowsweet, a natural source of salicylates)
- **"in"** = common suffix for medications at the time

Initially sold as a powder in glass bottles, aspirin was marketed to physicians for:
- Rheumatic fever and arthritis
- Headaches and neuralgias
- Fever reduction
- General pain relief

**Dosing**: One gram at a time—far higher than modern cardiovascular doses (75-325mg)

## Commercial Success

Aspirin became Bayer's flagship product almost immediately:
- By 1900, sold in 23 countries
- Generated massive profits, funding Bayer's expansion
- Entered the Guinness World Records in 1950 as the most frequently sold painkiller[^17]
- Became so ubiquitous that "aspirin" became a generic term in many countries (Bayer lost trademark rights post-WWI as part of war reparations)

## Summary: The Original Indication

Aspirin was designed, synthesized, tested, and marketed exclusively as an **analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory agent**—a better-tolerated alternative to salicylic acid for treating:
- Rheumatic fever and rheumatoid arthritis
- Headaches and body aches
- Fever
- Inflammatory conditions

**No one in 1897-1899 imagined aspirin could prevent cardiovascular disease.** That discovery would wait another 49 years and would come from an unexpected source: a small-town doctor watching his tonsillectomy patients bleed.
