# The Struggle: No Failure for the Original Indication

## A Different Kind of Story

Unlike many drug repurposing narratives, aspirin did **not fail** at its original indication. There was no "near-death" moment for the molecule, no bankruptcy threat, no regulatory rejection. Aspirin succeeded spectacularly as an analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory agent—and continued that success for over a century.

This dossier section is intentionally brief because aspirin's repurposing story follows a different arc: not rescue from failure, but **discovery of an entirely unanticipated capability** in an already successful drug.

## The Original Indication: Continued Success

From 1899 onward, aspirin dominated the pain relief market:

**Commercial triumph**:
- Became Bayer's flagship product within a year
- By 1950, recognized by Guinness World Records as the world's most frequently sold painkiller[^1]
- Generated enormous profits that funded Bayer's global expansion
- Became so ubiquitous the name "aspirin" became generic in many countries

**Clinical acceptance**:
- Rapidly adopted by physicians for rheumatic fever, arthritis, headaches
- Became the standard of care for inflammatory conditions
- Continued as first-line therapy even after acetaminophen (1956) and ibuprofen (1962) were introduced[^2]
- Remains a WHO Essential Medicine for pain and inflammation to this day

**Market dynamics**:
- Lost patent protection after World War I (seized as German war reparations)
- This actually *increased* aspirin's success—generic competition made it universally accessible and affordable
- Multiple manufacturers ensured stable supply and rock-bottom pricing

## The "Struggle": Not Failure, But Stagnation of Understanding

The real struggle with aspirin was not commercial or clinical failure—it was **intellectual stagnation**. For 74 years (1897-1971), no one understood *how* aspirin worked.

**Mystery mechanism** (1897-1971):
- Scientists knew aspirin reduced pain, fever, and inflammation
- The mechanism of action was completely unknown
- This didn't prevent clinical use—empirical effectiveness was sufficient
- But it limited innovation and prevented discovery of related benefits

**Consequences of ignorance**:
- Lawrence Craven's observations about cardiovascular effects (1948-1956) were dismissed partly because there was no plausible mechanism
- Without understanding prostaglandins and platelet function, Craven's bleeding-to-prevention hypothesis seemed speculative
- The medical establishment demanded mechanistic explanations before accepting new indications

**The breakthrough** finally came in 1971 when John Vane discovered that aspirin inhibits prostaglandin synthesis by blocking cyclooxygenase[^3]. This earned him the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1982. Suddenly, aspirin's diverse effects—and its cardiovascular potential—made biochemical sense.

## Side Effects: The Only Real "Failure"

The one area where aspirin showed problems was **tolerability**, though even this wasn't severe enough to threaten the drug:

**Gastric effects**:
- Stomach irritation, nausea (though far less than salicylic acid)
- Increased risk of gastrointestinal bleeding at high doses
- Ulcer formation with chronic use
- Led to development of enteric-coated formulations

**Other side effects**:
- Tinnitus (ringing in ears) at high doses
- Reye's syndrome in children (discovered 1963)—led to contraindication for pediatric fever
- Allergic reactions (aspirin sensitivity) in some patients

**Impact**: These side effects led to competition (acetaminophen, ibuprofen) but never threatened aspirin's market dominance. Instead, they ultimately helped reveal aspirin's cardiovascular benefits—because **bleeding complications** provided the clue that aspirin affected clotting.

## The Paradox: Success Blinded Science

Aspirin's very success at its original indication may have delayed recognition of its cardiovascular potential:

1. **Commercial satisfaction**: Bayer had a blockbuster drug; why look for other uses?
2. **Cognitive anchoring**: Physicians thought of aspirin as a "pain pill," not a cardiovascular agent
3. **Dose confusion**: Analgesic doses (500-1000mg) are far higher than cardioprotective doses (75-325mg)—using aspirin for pain masked any cardiovascular effects
4. **Side effect focus**: Medical attention focused on *preventing* bleeding complications, not exploiting them therapeutically

## The Craven Tragedy: A Personal "Near-Death"

While aspirin itself never faced a near-death moment, Lawrence Craven's hypothesis did—and Craven himself died tragically:

**Craven's observations** (1948-1956):
- Suburban California GP noticed excessive bleeding in tonsillectomy patients chewing aspirin gum
- Hypothesized that aspirin's blood-thinning effects could prevent heart attacks
- Prescribed daily aspirin to ~8,000 male patients aged 40-65
- Reported zero heart attacks in this cohort[^4]

**The establishment's rejection**:
- Published in obscure regional journals (Mississippi Valley Medical Journal)
- No randomized controlled trial—just observational data
- Medical community demanded rigorous proof before accepting such a revolutionary claim
- Craven's work was largely ignored and forgotten

**Craven's death** (1957):
- Died of a myocardial infarction at age 74
- Had stopped taking aspirin because he fell outside his own recommended age range (45-65)
- Never lived to see his hypothesis validated[^5]

This is perhaps the saddest "near-death" in the aspirin story: not the drug, but the idea—and the man—nearly lost to history.

## Summary

Aspirin's repurposing story is unusual because:
- **The original drug never struggled**—it succeeded continuously from 1899 onward
- **The struggle was epistemological**—understanding *why* it worked took 74 years
- **The cardiovascular discovery struggled**—Craven's observations were dismissed for decades
- **The personal tragedy**—Craven died of the very condition he'd shown aspirin could prevent

The lesson: Drug repurposing doesn't always emerge from failure. Sometimes the most important discoveries hide in plain sight within already successful medications. The challenge is not rescuing a failed drug—it's convincing a satisfied establishment to look beyond the known uses of a proven therapy.
