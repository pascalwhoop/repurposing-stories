# The Renaissance: Regulatory & Business Journey

## The Long Road: 1948-1985 (37 Years)

Unlike the original indication (aspirin was marketed within 2 years of synthesis), the cardiovascular application took **37 years from Craven's first observations (1948) to FDA approval (1985)**—and that approval came only after decades of trials, skepticism, and political pressure.

## Phase 1: The Wilderness Years (1948-1971)

**Craven's ignored publications** (1950-1956):
- No pharmaceutical company interest—aspirin was off-patent and generic
- No academic medical center took up the research
- No funding for randomized trials
- Craven died in 1957; his work was nearly forgotten[^1]

**Commercial reality**: Bayer and other manufacturers had no financial incentive to study aspirin's cardiovascular effects. Generic aspirin sold for pennies, and companies couldn't recoup the cost of expensive clinical trials. The system was designed to reward novel patented molecules, not repurposed generics.

**The skepticism**: The medical establishment viewed Craven's claims as implausible:
- Too simple (a common pain medication preventing heart attacks?)
- Too cheap (if aspirin worked, why develop expensive new drugs?)
- Too uncontrolled (observational data without randomization)
- No mechanism (aspirin's antiplatelet effects were unknown)

## Phase 2: Mechanistic Breakthrough (1960s-1971)

**The science catches up**:

**1963**: John O'Brien (haematologist, Portsmouth, UK) reported that 150mg of aspirin substantially reduced platelet aggregation[^2]

**1968**: Harvey Weiss found that aspirin had an anti-adhesive effect on blood platelets with low toxicity—making it a viable antiplatelet drug candidate[^3]

**1971**: **Sir John Vane** (Royal College of Surgeons, London) discovered that aspirin inhibits prostaglandin synthesis by blocking cyclooxygenase[^4]. Published in *Nature New Biology*:
- Dose-dependent inhibition of prostaglandin generation
- Mechanism explained aspirin's analgesic, antipyretic, and antiplatelet effects
- Provided biochemical rationale for cardiovascular use

**1971**: Smith and Willis confirmed that aspirin specifically inhibits prostaglandin synthesis in human platelets[^5]

**Impact**: Vane's discovery (for which he won the 1982 Nobel Prize) made aspirin's cardiovascular potential scientifically respectable. No longer a folk remedy—now a rational pharmacological intervention.

## Phase 3: The Clinical Trial Era (1974-1988)

### First Randomized Trial: Elwood (1974)

**Principal Investigator**: Dr. Peter Elwood, MRC Epidemiology Research Unit, Cardiff, Wales

**Design**:
- 1,239 men with recent myocardial infarction
- Randomized to aspirin vs. placebo
- Daily aspirin for secondary prevention
- Published in *British Medical Journal*, 1974[^6]

**Results**:
- 12% mortality reduction at 6 months
- 25% mortality reduction at 12 months
- **Not statistically significant** (underpowered study)
- But trends were encouraging enough to justify larger trials

**Historical significance**: The first rigorous RCT of aspirin for cardiovascular disease—23 years after Craven's first publication.

**The interruption**: During the trial, news of positive results in other aspirin studies leaked. Elwood's team had to break the randomization code prematurely, fearing ethical obligations. Despite small numbers showing promising trends, the early termination meant inconclusive results[^7].

### The Aspirin Myocardial Infarction Study (AMIS, 1980)

**Design**:
- 4,524 patients with previous MI
- Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled
- 1 gram aspirin daily
- Mean follow-up: 3 years

**Results**:
- 10% reduction in mortality (not statistically significant)
- Positive trend but failed to reach significance[^8]

**Problem**: Dose may have been too high (1000mg daily), causing gastric side effects and reducing compliance.

### The Turning Point: Physicians' Health Study (1989)

**Principal Investigator**: Dr. Charles H. Hennekens, Harvard Medical School/Brigham and Women's Hospital

**Design**:
- 22,071 apparently healthy male physicians
- Randomized to 325mg aspirin every other day vs. placebo
- **Primary prevention** (no prior cardiovascular disease)
- 2×2 factorial design (also tested beta-carotene)
- Entirely conducted by mail—novel, low-cost approach[^9]

**Results** (published *NEJM*, July 20, 1989):
- **44% reduction** in risk of first myocardial infarction (RR 0.56, p<0.00001)
- 254.8 vs. 439.7 MI per 100,000 person-years
- Slightly increased stroke risk (not statistically significant)
- No significant effect on cardiovascular mortality (too few events)
- Trial terminated early due to overwhelming efficacy[^10]

**Impact**: This was the breakthrough. A landmark trial in prestigious journal, massive effect size, primary prevention in healthy people. The evidence became impossible to ignore.

### The Definitive Trial: ISIS-2 (1988)

**Full name**: Second International Study of Infarct Survival

**Design**:
- **17,187 patients** with suspected acute myocardial infarction
- Randomized to four groups: streptokinase, aspirin, both, or neither
- 160mg daily aspirin for one month
- Multicentered (417 hospitals)
- Randomization began March 1985; completed December 1987[^11]

**Results** (published *Lancet*, 1988):
- **Aspirin alone**: 23% reduction in 5-week vascular mortality (9.4% vs. 11.8%, p<0.00001)
- **Streptokinase alone**: 25% reduction in mortality
- **Combination (aspirin + streptokinase)**: 42% reduction in mortality (8.0% vs. 13.2%)
- Effects were **additive**—aspirin and thrombolytics worked independently
- Benefit even when started 13-24 hours after symptom onset
- Absolute benefit: **25 lives saved per 1,000 patients treated**[^12]

**Significance**: This trial was unequivocal. The effect size, statistical power, and clinical magnitude were overwhelming. ISIS-2 transformed clinical practice worldwide within months.

### Meta-Analysis: The Final Word

**Antiplatelet Trialists' Collaboration** (*Lancet*, 2009):
- Meta-analysis of individual participant data from randomized trials
- 16 secondary prevention trials (17,000 patients)
- 6 primary prevention trials (95,000 patients)[^13]

**Findings**:
- **Secondary prevention**: 20-25% reduction in serious vascular events
- **Primary prevention**: 12% reduction in serious vascular events
- Bleeding risks: ~0.03% major extracranial bleeds per year
- Benefits clearly outweighed risks for secondary prevention

## Phase 4: Regulatory Approval

### FDA Decision (1985)

**Timeline**:
- **1985**: FDA approved professional labeling of aspirin for secondary prevention of myocardial infarction[^14]
- Based on accumulating evidence from multiple trials
- Indication: "To reduce risk of death and/or non-fatal MI in patients with previous MI or unstable angina pectoris"

**Dose recommendation**: 75-325mg daily (far lower than analgesic doses of 500-1000mg)

**Key approvals**:
1. **Secondary prevention of MI** (1985)
2. **Reduction of TIA and stroke** in patients with prior TIA (1980s)
3. **Primary prevention** (never formally approved by FDA, but recommended by USPSTF for select high-risk individuals)

### The Cardio-Renal Drugs Advisory Committee Controversy

**1988**: The FDA's Cardio-Renal Drugs Advisory Committee reviewed the Physicians' Health Study and recommended approval of professional labeling for **primary prevention** (aspirin to prevent first MI in healthy people)[^15].

**FDA's response**: The agency did **not act** on this recommendation. Why?
- Only one positive trial (Physicians' Health Study)
- British Doctors' Trial (5,139 men) showed no significant benefit
- Concern about promoting aspirin use in millions of healthy people
- Bleeding risk considerations
- Lack of effect on mortality (only non-fatal MI reduction)[^16]

**Resolution**: Primary prevention remained controversial. Current guidelines (2019-2022) recommend *against* routine aspirin for primary prevention, due to bleeding risks and improved alternative therapies (statins, blood pressure control)[^17].

## Business & Market Dynamics

### The Generic Problem

**No patent protection**:
- Aspirin went off-patent post-WWI
- Multiple generic manufacturers
- Razor-thin profit margins (pennies per pill)

**Who paid for trials?**
- UK Medical Research Council (government-funded research)
- US National Institutes of Health
- Pharmaceutical companies had minimal incentive—couldn't recoup trial costs

**The paradox**: Aspirin's cardiovascular repurposing succeeded *despite* lack of commercial incentive, not because of it. Publicly funded research drove the discovery.

### Market Impact Post-Approval

**Sales resurgence**:
- Aspirin sales had been declining (1960s-1970s) due to competition from acetaminophen and ibuprofen
- Cardiovascular indication revived the market
- By 1990s, aspirin sales surged due to preventive use[^18]

**Current market** (2024):
- Global aspirin market: **$2.5-3.4 billion annually**
- 50-120 billion pills consumed per year
- Leading manufacturers: Bayer, Perrigo, generic producers
- Growth driver: cardiovascular disease prevention in aging populations[^19]

### Bayer's Strategy

**Brand differentiation**:
- Bayer marketed branded "Bayer Aspirin" for cardiovascular use
- Premium pricing despite generic competition
- Emphasized purity, consistency, and trusted brand
- Developed enteric-coated formulations (Bayer Aspirin Cardio)

**Outcome**: Bayer maintained market leadership despite losing patent exclusivity decades earlier.

## The Skepticism: Why Did It Take So Long?

**Institutional barriers**:
1. **Publication bias**: Craven's findings in obscure journals
2. **Funding gaps**: No commercial sponsor for generic drug trials
3. **Methodological standards**: Observational data insufficient; demanded RCTs
4. **Cognitive inertia**: Aspirin seen as "just a pain pill"
5. **Economic incentives**: System rewards novel patented drugs, not repurposed generics

**The 37-year timeline**:
- 1948: Craven's first observations
- 1974: First RCT (inconclusive)
- 1985: FDA approval for secondary prevention
- 1988-1989: Definitive trials (ISIS-2, Physicians' Health Study)

**The tragedy**: Millions of preventable deaths during those 37 years while the evidence slowly accumulated.

## Summary: The Renaissance

Aspirin's cardiovascular renaissance required:
- A mechanistic breakthrough (Vane, 1971) to make the hypothesis scientifically plausible
- Publicly funded clinical trials (no commercial incentive)
- 37 years of accumulating evidence from multiple RCTs
- Definitive mega-trials (ISIS-2, Physicians' Health Study) to overcome skepticism
- FDA approval (1985) that came only after overwhelming data

The journey from Craven's garage observations to global standard of care demonstrates the immense friction in repurposing generic drugs. Without patents, without profit, the research infrastructure nearly failed to pursue a therapy that would save millions of lives.

Aspirin succeeded despite the system, not because of it.
