# The Bleeding Clue

ELENA: [energetic] Alright, we're back. And we need to talk about Lawrence Craven.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Dr. Lawrence Craven. General practitioner in Glendale, California. Not a cardiologist, not at a major academic center. Just a suburban GP.

ELENA: [intrigued] Which is important, because this is about to be a story about the establishment ignoring someone who wasn't part of the club.

MARCUS: [serious] In 1943, Craven is performing routine tonsillectomies. It's a common procedure—kids get their tonsils out, recover in a few days, go home.

ELENA: [curious] But he starts noticing a pattern.

MARCUS: [analytical] Some of his patients are bleeding excessively after surgery. Not normal post-operative bleeding—this is persistent, sometimes requiring re-intervention.

ELENA: [fascinated] And Craven is a good clinician. He starts asking questions. What do these patients have in common?

MARCUS: [excited] They were all chewing Aspergum.

ELENA: [surprised] Wait, Aspergum? Like aspirin chewing gum?

MARCUS: [amused] Yes. This was the 1940s. Aspergum was marketed as a convenient way to take aspirin for pain. Kids especially liked it because you didn't have to swallow pills.

ELENA: [laughing] The original gummy vitamin concept.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] But Craven realized—aspirin was causing the bleeding. Or more precisely, aspirin was preventing normal blood clotting.

ELENA: [intrigued] Now, most doctors would stop there. "Aspirin causes bleeding, stop giving it before surgery, publish a warning, move on."

MARCUS: [impressed] But Craven made a different leap.

ELENA: [excited] He asked a different question.

MARCUS: [passionate] If aspirin prevents clotting enough to cause post-operative hemorrhage, could it prevent the coronary clots that cause heart attacks?

ELENA: [amazed] That's brilliant. That's the kind of lateral thinking that changes medicine.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] And to understand why this was so radical, we need to talk about what medicine knew—or thought it knew—about heart attacks in the 1940s.

ELENA: [curious] What was the prevailing theory?

MARCUS: [analytical] It was actually still debated. The thrombotic theory—that heart attacks are caused by blood clots blocking coronary arteries—was gaining acceptance, but not everyone believed it.

ELENA: [fascinated] What were the alternatives?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Some thought heart attacks were caused by coronary artery spasms. Others thought it was a metabolic problem with the heart muscle itself. The idea that clots were the primary cause wasn't settled science yet.

ELENA: [impressed] So Craven is betting on a theory that's not even universally accepted.

MARCUS: [reflective] Right. But he's convinced. And here's where it gets really interesting—he decides to test his hypothesis.

ELENA: [excited] On his own patients.

MARCUS: [serious] Starting in 1948, Craven begins recommending daily aspirin to his male patients between the ages of 45 and 65.

ELENA: [curious] Why only men?

MARCUS: [analytical] Heart attacks in the 1940s were predominantly seen as a male problem. The gender gap in cardiovascular disease was much more pronounced before women started smoking at similar rates and before we understood hormonal protection.

ELENA: [thoughtful] And 45 to 65—that's prime heart attack territory.

MARCUS: [impressed] Craven was targeting the highest-risk population. And he's tracking them carefully, year after year.

ELENA: [intrigued] What dose was he using?

MARCUS: [excited] This is fascinating. He's not using the low doses we use today—75 to 100 milligrams. He's using 300 to 600 milligrams daily.

ELENA: [surprised] That's much higher.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Because remember, in 1948, nobody understood aspirin's mechanism. Nobody knew about COX-1 inhibition or antiplatelet effects. Craven was working purely from observation.

ELENA: [analytical] He's essentially running an uncontrolled observational study in his general practice.

MARCUS: [serious] For eight years.

ELENA: [amazed] Eight years of following these patients. And what did he find?

MARCUS: [passionate] Zero heart attacks.

ELENA: [shocked] Wait, zero?

MARCUS: [emphatic] Zero. He published his first report in 1950 in the Mississippi Valley Medical Journal. 400 patients, daily aspirin, no coronary or cerebral thromboses.

ELENA: [excited] That's extraordinary.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] He continued the study. By 1953, he had 1,500 patients. By 1956, over 8,000 patients.

ELENA: [amazed] Eight thousand patients, and he's reporting--

MARCUS: [intense] "Not a single case of detectable coronary or cerebral thrombosis."

ELENA: [stunned] Not one.

MARCUS: [reflective] Now, let's be clear about what this means statistically. In the late 1940s and 1950s, cardiovascular disease was the leading cause of death in American men.

ELENA: [analytical] So in a cohort of 8,000 men aged 45 to 65, followed for multiple years, you would expect--

MARCUS: [serious] Hundreds of events. Conservatively, you'd expect 150 to 300 heart attacks and strokes over that time period.

ELENA: [passionate] And Craven is seeing zero.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Or at least, he's reporting zero. There are questions about his methodology—no placebo control, self-reported outcomes, potential selection bias.

ELENA: [skeptical] But even if we're generous and say he missed some events, the reduction he's describing is massive.

MARCUS: [emphatic] It's one of the most dramatic clinical observations in the history of medicine.

ELENA: [frustrated] So obviously, the medical establishment immediately funded large-scale trials to confirm his findings, right?

MARCUS: [somber] No.

ELENA: [shocked] No?

MARCUS: [sad] Dead silence.

ELENA: [disbelieving] He publishes data on 8,000 patients showing what could be the greatest preventive therapy in cardiovascular medicine, and nobody follows up?

MARCUS: [reflective] Nobody. No NIH grants. No academic medical centers launching trials. No pharmaceutical companies interested.

ELENA: [passionate] This is insane. Why was he dismissed?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Let's break it down. There were several barriers.

ELENA: [analytical] First, the publication venue.

MARCUS: [serious] Mississippi Valley Medical Journal. Regional, not prestigious, not widely read by the cardiology elite.

ELENA: [frustrated] If he'd published in JAMA or the New England Journal of Medicine--

MARCUS: [somber] He tried. They rejected him.

ELENA: [angry] Of course they did.

MARCUS: [reflective] Second, the methodology. No randomization, no placebo control, no blinding. By academic standards, it was "unscientific."

ELENA: [skeptical] Even though he had 8,000 patients.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Sample size doesn't overcome design flaws in the eyes of rigorous researchers. And they weren't wrong to be skeptical—observational studies can mislead.

ELENA: [contemplative] Fair point. What else?

MARCUS: [analytical] Third, selection bias. Craven's patients weren't randomly selected—they were his patients, presumably healthier or more compliant than the general population.

ELENA: [intrigued] The worried well.

MARCUS: [impressed] Exactly. Patients who see their doctor regularly, follow recommendations, probably have better health behaviors overall.

ELENA: [thoughtful] So the results could be confounded.

MARCUS: [serious] Fourth, the results were too good to be true. Zero events in 8,000 patients? That strains credulity.

ELENA: [frustrated] But what if it was true?

MARCUS: [reflective] Then it would mean that a drug discovered in 1897, sold over the counter for 50 years, could prevent the leading cause of death. And nobody had noticed.

ELENA: [passionate] Which is embarrassing.

MARCUS: [somber] Fifth, there was no mechanism. Nobody knew how aspirin worked, let alone how it could prevent heart attacks.

ELENA: [analytical] And medicine in the 1950s was becoming more rigorous, more scientific. Empirical observations without mechanistic explanations were viewed with suspicion.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] And sixth—maybe the most important—aspirin was cheap.

ELENA: [excited] Here it is. The economic barrier.

MARCUS: [serious] Aspirin went off-patent after World War I as part of German war reparations. By 1948, it was a generic drug selling for pennies.

ELENA: [frustrated] So no pharmaceutical company would fund trials to prove Craven right, because even if aspirin worked, they couldn't make money from it.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Negative return on investment. Guaranteed.

ELENA: [passionate] This is the market failure at the heart of the aspirin story.

MARCUS: [reflective] All six barriers reinforced each other. Wrong venue, wrong methodology, wrong result magnitude, no mechanism, establishment skepticism, no commercial sponsor.

ELENA: [somber] And Lawrence Craven kept practicing medicine in Glendale, California, convinced he was right but unable to prove it to anyone who mattered.

MARCUS: [sad] In 1957, Lawrence Craven died of a heart attack at age 74.

ELENA: [shocked] The very thing he was trying to prevent.

MARCUS: [somber] Some accounts suggest he had stopped taking aspirin himself—possibly because at 74, he fell outside his own recommended age range of 45 to 65.

ELENA: [emotional] No.

MARCUS: [reflective] He never knew his hypothesis would eventually be proven correct. He never saw aspirin save a single life from cardiovascular disease.

ELENA: [passionate] That is heartbreaking.

MARCUS: [serious] But his publications remained in the medical literature. Obscure, ignored, but there.

ELENA: [hopeful] Waiting for someone to take them seriously.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] The question is—how long would medicine take to listen?

ELENA: [determined] And what would it take to change their minds?
