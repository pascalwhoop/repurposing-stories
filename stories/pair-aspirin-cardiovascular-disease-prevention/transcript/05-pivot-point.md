# The Mechanism Breakthrough

ELENA: [energetic] Alright, we're back. And we need to talk about the breakthrough that changed everything.

MARCUS: [excited] 1971. John Vane at the Royal College of Surgeons in London.

ELENA: [intrigued] He wasn't even studying aspirin, was he?

MARCUS: [amused] No. He was investigating prostaglandins—these mysterious signaling molecules involved in inflammation and pain.

ELENA: [curious] And he stumbles onto aspirin?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] He's testing how different compounds affect prostaglandin synthesis. He adds aspirin to his system, and boom—prostaglandin production shuts down almost completely.

ELENA: [amazed] Just like that.

MARCUS: [analytical] He discovers that aspirin blocks an enzyme called cyclooxygenase—COX. Specifically COX-1, which platelets use to make thromboxane A2.

ELENA: [intrigued] Which is the clotting signal.

MARCUS: [excited] Exactly. Thromboxane A2 tells platelets to stick together and form clots. Block COX-1, and you block clot formation.

ELENA: [impressed] So suddenly, Craven's observation makes biochemical sense.

MARCUS: [passionate] After 23 years, there's finally a mechanism. And here's the elegant part: aspirin's inhibition is irreversible for platelets.

ELENA: [curious] Why does that matter?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Platelets can't make new enzymes—they don't have a nucleus. Once aspirin acetylates their COX-1, that platelet can't clot for its entire 7-10 day lifespan.

ELENA: [fascinated] So one dose affects your platelets for a week.

MARCUS: [delighted] Which is why such a low dose works. You don't need to inhibit every platelet every moment—you're cumulatively reducing clotting ability over time.

ELENA: [thoughtful] And this explained the dose. Craven was using 300 to 600 milligrams, but now we know 75 to 100 is enough.

MARCUS: [reflective] Understanding the mechanism made the therapy better. Lower dose, fewer side effects, same cardiovascular benefit.

ELENA: [excited] And in 1982, Vane wins the Nobel Prize.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Overnight, aspirin goes from "generic painkiller" to "Nobel Prize-validated antiplatelet therapy."

ELENA: [analytical] Scientific respectability achieved.

MARCUS: [somber] But Craven had been dead for 14 years.

ELENA: [frustrated] Twenty-three years from his first publication to the mechanism discovery. And during that gap—

MARCUS: [serious] Millions of preventable deaths. Because medicine wouldn't accept empirical evidence without mechanistic understanding.

ELENA: [passionate] So Vane's discovery didn't just explain aspirin. It broke the logjam.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] It made aspirin fundable. Made the trials possible.

ELENA: [excited] So now it's the early 1980s. We have the observation. We have the mechanism. We have a Nobel Prize.

MARCUS: [hopeful] Surely pharmaceutical companies are lining up to fund trials, right?

ELENA: [knowing] Let me guess. No.

MARCUS: [somber] Not even close. Because the next barrier was about to stop everything cold.

ELENA: [curious] Economics?

MARCUS: [emphatic] The most expensive market failure in pharmaceutical history.
