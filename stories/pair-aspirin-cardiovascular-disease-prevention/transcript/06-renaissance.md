# The Trial Gauntlet

ELENA: [serious] Okay, let's talk about the trials. Because this is where the economics of drug repurposing get really dark.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] It's 1971. We have the mechanism. We know aspirin inhibits platelet aggregation. The hypothesis is scientifically credible.

ELENA: [frustrated] So pharmaceutical companies should be lining up to fund cardiovascular trials, right?

MARCUS: [somber] Wrong. Nobody will touch it.

ELENA: [passionate] Why? This is a drug that could prevent the leading cause of death. The market is enormous.

MARCUS: [analytical] The market exists. But the profit doesn't. Remember, aspirin went off-patent in 1919 as part of German war reparations after World War I.

ELENA: [realizing] So by 1971, aspirin is fully generic.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Fully generic. Hundreds of manufacturers. Commodity pricing. A bottle of 100 aspirin tablets costs maybe a dollar retail.

ELENA: [calculating] So one cent per pill. And at that price, there's no way to recoup the cost of clinical trials.

MARCUS: [serious] Cardiovascular outcome trials are expensive. You need thousands of patients, followed for years, with hard endpoints like death and myocardial infarction.

ELENA: [curious] What's the price tag?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] In the 1970s and 1980s, a definitive cardiovascular trial would cost $10 million to $50 million minimum.

ELENA: [amazed] And there's no way to make that back selling generic aspirin.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Impossible. Even if aspirin worked perfectly, even if every cardiologist in the world prescribed it, the profit margin on generic aspirin is so thin that no company could justify the investment.

ELENA: [frustrated] This is a market failure.

MARCUS: [reflective] It's the most expensive market failure in pharmaceutical history. Because the social benefit is astronomical—saving millions of lives, preventing billions in healthcare costs—but the private return on investment is negative.

ELENA: [analytical] So who pays for the trials?

MARCUS: [serious] Governments. Public funding. Taxpayers.

ELENA: [intrigued] Walk me through it.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] The first proper randomized controlled trial was led by Peter Elwood at the Medical Research Council in Cardiff, Wales. Published in 1974.

ELENA: [excited] UK government-funded.

MARCUS: [impressed] Right. Elwood enrolled about 1,200 men who'd already had a heart attack and randomized them to aspirin or placebo.

ELENA: [curious] What were the results?

MARCUS: [contemplative] Promising but not definitive. There was a trend toward fewer deaths and fewer subsequent heart attacks in the aspirin group, but the trial was underpowered.

ELENA: [analytical] Not enough patients to reach statistical significance.

MARCUS: [serious] Exactly. Elwood showed that a trial was feasible, that aspirin was safe, and that there might be a benefit. But he couldn't prove it conclusively.

ELENA: [frustrated] So more trials were needed.

MARCUS: [somber] Many more. The 1970s and early 1980s were the wilderness years. Multiple trials, different designs, mixed results.

ELENA: [curious] What were the sticking points?

MARCUS: [analytical] Sample size, mostly. Cardiovascular events take time to accumulate. If you don't have enough patients or don't follow them long enough, you won't see a statistically significant difference.

ELENA: [thoughtful] And every trial costs millions of dollars that nobody wants to spend on a generic drug.

MARCUS: [reflective] Right. So you get underpowered trials that give ambiguous results, which paradoxically makes it harder to justify funding the large, definitive trial you actually need.

ELENA: [intrigued] So what changed? What broke the logjam?

MARCUS: [excited] Two trials in the late 1980s. Both massive. Both publicly funded.

ELENA: [leaning in] Tell me.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] The first was the Physicians' Health Study in the United States. Started in 1982, published in 1989.

ELENA: [curious] How big?

MARCUS: [impressed] 22,071 male physicians. Randomized to aspirin or placebo. Followed for five years.

ELENA: [amazed] That's huge.

MARCUS: [excited] And the results were stunning. Aspirin reduced the risk of first myocardial infarction by 44%.

ELENA: [shocked] Forty-four percent?

MARCUS: [emphatic] Almost half. In healthy men with no prior history of cardiovascular disease, one aspirin tablet every other day cut the heart attack risk nearly in half.

ELENA: [passionate] That's primary prevention. Preventing the first event.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Right. But even more important was the second trial: ISIS-2.

ELENA: [intrigued] ISIS-2. I've heard this is the big one.

MARCUS: [serious] This is the trial that changed everything. Second International Study of Infarct Survival. Led by British researchers, coordinated by Oxford.

ELENA: [curious] When was it?

MARCUS: [analytical] Enrollment from 1985 to 1987. Results published in 1988 in The Lancet.

ELENA: [excited] What was the design?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] This was brilliant. They enrolled 17,187 patients presenting with acute myocardial infarction.

ELENA: [clarifying] People actively having a heart attack.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Yes. Within 24 hours of symptom onset. Randomized to four groups: aspirin, streptokinase—a clot-busting drug—both, or neither.

ELENA: [impressed] Factorial design. Testing two interventions and their combination in one trial.

MARCUS: [excited] And the results were definitive. Aspirin alone reduced vascular mortality by 23%. Streptokinase alone reduced mortality by 25%.

ELENA: [amazed] And the combination?

MARCUS: [passionate] Additive. Aspirin plus streptokinase reduced mortality by 42%.

ELENA: [stunned] That's one of the largest mortality reductions I've ever heard of in cardiology.

MARCUS: [reflective] In absolute terms: 25 lives saved per 1,000 patients treated with aspirin during acute MI.

ELENA: [calculating] So if you treat 40 patients with aspirin during a heart attack, you save one life.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Number needed to treat: 40. That's extraordinary for a cardiovascular intervention.

ELENA: [curious] How much did ISIS-2 cost?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Remarkably little. About $2 million. They used a "large simple trial" design—minimal data collection, pragmatic approach, enrollment across 417 hospitals.

ELENA: [impressed] So for $2 million, they generated the definitive evidence that aspirin saves lives.

MARCUS: [reflective] Compare that to the billions in healthcare costs saved. The return on investment is almost incalculable.

ELENA: [analytical] And what happened after ISIS-2?

MARCUS: [excited] Overnight transformation. Within months, aspirin became standard of care for acute myocardial infarction worldwide.

ELENA: [amazed] That fast?

MARCUS: [emphatic] The evidence was so overwhelming that practice changed immediately. Cardiologists around the world started giving aspirin to every heart attack patient.

ELENA: [thoughtful] What about regulatory approval?

MARCUS: [analytical] The FDA had actually approved aspirin for secondary prevention of cardiovascular events in 1985, based on accumulating evidence from earlier trials.

ELENA: [curious] Before ISIS-2?

MARCUS: [impressed] Yes. The FDA looked at meta-analyses of multiple smaller trials and concluded there was sufficient evidence for secondary prevention—preventing a second heart attack in people who'd already had one.

ELENA: [reflective] So the approval came first, but ISIS-2 was the trial that cemented the evidence and changed practice globally.

MARCUS: [serious] Right. And here's the timeline: Craven's first publication was 1950. FDA approval was 1985.

ELENA: [somber] Thirty-five years.

MARCUS: [frustrated] Thirty-five years from observation to regulatory approval. And even then, it took ISIS-2 in 1988 to make aspirin universally accepted.

ELENA: [passionate] Let's put that in human terms. Cardiovascular disease was killing—and still is—millions of people per year.

MARCUS: [analytical] If aspirin had been approved and widely prescribed in 1960 instead of 1985—25 years earlier—the number of lives saved is in the millions.

ELENA: [calculating] Conservatively, 5 to 10 million preventable deaths during that gap.

MARCUS: [somber] That's the cost of the delay. Not just Craven's tragedy, but millions of people who died of heart attacks that aspirin could have prevented.

ELENA: [reflective] And the reason for the delay was primarily economic. No company would fund the trials because aspirin was generic.

MARCUS: [emphatic] So publicly funded research had to do it. The UK Medical Research Council, the NIH—taxpayer money saved millions of lives that the pharmaceutical industry wouldn't invest in.

ELENA: [thoughtful] There's a broader lesson here about drug repurposing.

MARCUS: [curious] What's that?

ELENA: [analytical] When a potential therapy involves a generic drug, market forces fail. The social benefit can be enormous, but the private return is negative. Public funding becomes essential.

MARCUS: [reflective] And public funding is slower, smaller, and competes with other priorities.

ELENA: [frustrated] Which means life-saving discoveries get delayed by decades.

MARCUS: [serious] That's the aspirin story in a nutshell. Brilliant observation, elegant mechanism, definitive trials—but a 40-year gap from start to finish.

ELENA: [determined] So ISIS-2 happens in 1988. Aspirin is now standard of care for heart attacks. The story's over, right?

MARCUS: [somber] The efficacy story is over. The impact story? That's just beginning.

ELENA: [intrigued] What do you mean?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Because proving a drug works and actually getting it to the people who need it are two very different challenges.

ELENA: [excited] Implementation.

MARCUS: [serious] And that's where the story gets complicated. Shall we take one more break?

ELENA: [agreeing] Let's do it. We'll be right back.
