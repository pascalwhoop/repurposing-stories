# The Paradox of Impact

ELENA: [energetic] Alright, we're back. And we need to talk about the impact—the real-world, global-scale impact of aspirin for cardiovascular prevention.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] This is where the story gets bittersweet. Because the numbers are extraordinary, but there's also this persistent gap between what's possible and what's actually happening.

ELENA: [curious] Let's start with the success. How many lives has aspirin saved since ISIS-2?

MARCUS: [analytical] The estimates vary, but most researchers agree: somewhere between 20 and 35 million cardiovascular events prevented since 1988.

ELENA: [amazed] Twenty to thirty-five million.

MARCUS: [reflective] Heart attacks and strokes that didn't happen because people took aspirin.

ELENA: [passionate] That's more than the population of Australia.

MARCUS: [impressed] And currently, aspirin prevents an estimated 500,000 to over 1 million cardiovascular events annually.

ELENA: [stunned] Every year. One million people who don't have a heart attack or stroke because of a drug discovered in 1897.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] And the absolute benefit is massive. ISIS-2 showed 25 lives saved per 1,000 patients treated during acute MI. Secondary prevention studies show a 20-25% reduction in recurrent events.

ELENA: [analytical] So we're not talking about marginal benefits. This is a quarter reduction in cardiovascular mortality.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Right. And the drug costs essentially nothing.

ELENA: [excited] Let's talk economics. What's the cost-effectiveness?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] This is where aspirin becomes almost absurd. Cost-effectiveness analyses estimate less than $100 per quality-adjusted life-year gained.

ELENA: [shocked] Under $100 per QALY?

MARCUS: [amused] Some studies put it under $50.

ELENA: [laughing] That's not even in the same universe as most medical interventions. The standard threshold for cost-effectiveness is $50,000 to $100,000 per QALY.

MARCUS: [reflective] Aspirin is three orders of magnitude better.

ELENA: [calculating] And what's the return on investment for healthcare systems?

MARCUS: [analytical] Aspirin prevents acute coronary events that cost $50,000 to $100,000 to treat. Aspirin costs maybe $10 to $18 per year.

ELENA: [amazed] So every dollar spent on aspirin saves $50 to $300 in healthcare costs.

MARCUS: [emphatic] That's a 5,000% to 30,000% ROI.

ELENA: [passionate] No wonder it's on the WHO Essential Medicines List.

MARCUS: [serious] It should be a no-brainer. Universal aspirin therapy for everyone with established cardiovascular disease or high risk.

ELENA: [somber] But that's not what's happening, is it?

MARCUS: [frustrated] No. And this is where the story turns tragic again.

ELENA: [curious] What's the actual uptake?

MARCUS: [somber] Globally, only about 40% of people with established cardiovascular disease are taking aspirin.

ELENA: [shocked] Forty percent?

MARCUS: [emphatic] That means 60% of people who should be taking aspirin—people who've had heart attacks, strokes, diagnosed coronary artery disease—aren't getting it.

ELENA: [frustrated] Why?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] In high-income countries, it's a mix of physician reluctance—worrying about bleeding risks—and patient non-adherence. People stop taking it, or they never start.

ELENA: [analytical] What about low-income countries?

MARCUS: [somber] That's where it gets really grim. In low-income countries, only 16.6% of eligible patients receive aspirin.

ELENA: [angry] One in six.

MARCUS: [passionate] This is a drug that costs pennies. It doesn't require refrigeration, it's stable for years, it's easy to manufacture. And 83% of people who need it in the poorest countries aren't getting it.

ELENA: [reflective] That's not an economic barrier—aspirin is universally affordable.

MARCUS: [serious] It's a healthcare systems barrier. Lack of diagnosis, lack of physician training, lack of pharmaceutical supply chains, lack of patient education.

ELENA: [frustrated] So we've proven the drug works. We've made it essentially free. And we're still failing to get it to most of the people who need it.

MARCUS: [somber] That's the implementation gap. And it's costing hundreds of thousands of lives every year.

ELENA: [thoughtful] Are there risks we need to talk about? Because I know bleeding is a concern.

MARCUS: [analytical] Absolutely. Aspirin increases the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding and hemorrhagic stroke.

ELENA: [curious] How significant?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] For secondary prevention—people who've already had a cardiovascular event—the benefit vastly outweighs the risk. You might increase bleeding risk by 0.1 to 0.3% per year, but you're reducing cardiovascular events by 20-25%.

ELENA: [intrigued] What about primary prevention? Healthy people with no cardiovascular history?

MARCUS: [serious] That's where the risk-benefit calculation flips. In 2022, the US Preventive Services Task Force recommended *against* routine aspirin for primary prevention.

ELENA: [surprised] They reversed the guidelines?

MARCUS: [reflective] Yes. The data showed that in low-risk populations—people who've never had a heart attack—the bleeding risk approaches or exceeds the cardiovascular benefit.

ELENA: [analytical] So aspirin is incredibly effective for secondary prevention but not recommended for primary prevention anymore.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Right. The lesson is: aspirin works brilliantly for people at high risk, but it's not a universal preventive for everyone.

ELENA: [thoughtful] That seems like a reasonable, evidence-based distinction.

MARCUS: [agreeing] It is. But it also means the target population for aspirin is narrower than we once thought.

ELENA: [curious] What about the cultural legacy? Aspirin is the most recognizable medication on the planet.

MARCUS: [amused] It's iconic. Everyone knows aspirin. It's in medicine cabinets worldwide.

ELENA: [excited] And it inspired the entire field of drug repurposing, didn't it?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Absolutely. Aspirin proved that old drugs can have transformative new uses. It opened the door for researchers to look at other generics with fresh eyes.

ELENA: [intrigued] Like what?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Metformin for PCOS and longevity research. Colchicine for cardiovascular disease. Dexamethasone for COVID-19.

ELENA: [impressed] The RECOVERY trial in the UK—testing generic dexamethasone during the pandemic—was directly inspired by the aspirin model.

MARCUS: [reflective] Right. Publicly funded, pragmatic, large-scale trial of a cheap generic drug. And it worked. Dexamethasone reduced COVID mortality by 35% in severe cases.

ELENA: [passionate] So aspirin's legacy isn't just the lives it saved directly—it's the model it created for how to repurpose generics.

MARCUS: [serious] But here's the frustrating part: we still haven't fixed the systemic problems that delayed aspirin for 37 years.

ELENA: [curious] What do you mean?

MARCUS: [analytical] The FDA still doesn't have a streamlined pathway for generic drug repurposing. The NIH doesn't have dedicated funding for it. Every generic repurposing candidate still faces the same barriers aspirin did.

ELENA: [frustrated] So we learned the lesson but didn't change the system.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Exactly. Aspirin proved that publicly funded research can save millions of lives when markets fail. But we haven't institutionalized that lesson.

ELENA: [reflective] That's a policy failure.

MARCUS: [serious] And an ongoing tragedy. Because there are probably other aspirin-like stories out there—cheap generics with untapped potential—that we're not investigating because there's no commercial incentive and no systematic public funding.

ELENA: [thoughtful] So let's step back. We've been through this whole story. What are the lessons?

MARCUS: [contemplative] For me, the biggest lesson is about observation. Lawrence Craven noticed something in his routine practice—excessive bleeding—and instead of just treating it as a complication, he asked a different question.

ELENA: [intrigued] "What does this tell me about what aspirin does?"

MARCUS: [impressed] Exactly. That cognitive shift—from problem-solving to hypothesis-generation—is the hallmark of observational breakthroughs.

ELENA: [analytical] And the second lesson is about mechanism. Medicine needed Vane's discovery to take Craven seriously. The 23-year gap from observation to mechanism cost millions of lives.

MARCUS: [reflective] Right. But here's the tension: should we require mechanistic understanding before approving therapies, or should empirical evidence be enough for low-risk interventions?

ELENA: [thoughtful] That's the fundamental question. Aspirin was safe—it had been used for 50 years. The bleeding risk was known and manageable. Could we have approved it in the 1960s based on Craven's data, even without Vane's mechanism?

MARCUS: [contemplative] Maybe. But without mechanism, there's no dose optimization, no understanding of who benefits most, no rational combination therapies.

ELENA: [analytical] Fair point. The third lesson is about economics. The public goods problem.

MARCUS: [emphatic] This is the killer. Pharmaceutical markets reward novel patented drugs, not repurposed generics. When a life-saving discovery involves a generic, market forces fail.

ELENA: [passionate] And that means public funding is essential. Governments have to step in.

MARCUS: [serious] But public funding is slower, smaller, and competes with other priorities. Which is why it took 37 years from Craven to FDA approval.

ELENA: [frustrated] That delay cost an estimated 5 to 10 million lives.

MARCUS: [somber] And even today, only 40% of people who need aspirin are getting it. The drug works. The system fails.

ELENA: [reflective] So aspirin is simultaneously medicine's greatest triumph and its most damning indictment.

MARCUS: [agreeing] Perfectly put. The cheapest, most effective cardiovascular intervention ever discovered—but a clinician saw the truth in 1948, and the establishment took 40 years to listen.

ELENA: [determined] And millions of people who should be taking it today still aren't.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] If there's a call to action from this story, it's this: listen to clinicians, fund mechanistic research, invest in public-good trials, and fix the implementation gap.

ELENA: [passionate] Because the next aspirin might already be sitting in someone's medicine cabinet, waiting for the right observer to notice.

MARCUS: [serious] And we can't afford to wait another 40 years.
