# The Playbook: Mental Models

ELENA: [energetic] Alright, let's shift gears. We've told the story. Now let's extract the mental models—the patterns that show up in aspirin and might apply to other drug repurposing stories.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] I love this part. Because aspirin isn't just a historical curiosity—it's a template for how medical breakthroughs happen and why they get delayed.

ELENA: [curious] So what's the first model?

MARCUS: [excited] The Observer's Advantage. Clinical serendipity meets the prepared mind.

ELENA: [intrigued] Walk me through it.

MARCUS: [analytical] The pattern is this: breakthrough discoveries often emerge not from hypothesis-driven research in elite labs, but from alert clinicians noticing unexpected patterns in routine practice.

ELENA: [thoughtful] Lawrence Craven performing tonsillectomies.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Exactly. He had a problem—patients bleeding excessively. The conventional response would be: "Stop recommending Aspergum, publish a warning, move on."

ELENA: [excited] But Craven asked a different question.

MARCUS: [passionate] "What does this tell me about what aspirin does to blood?" That cognitive shift—from problem-solving to hypothesis-generation—is everything.

ELENA: [impressed] And it required knowledge synthesis. He connected surgical bleeding, thrombotic theory of MI, and aspirin's unknown mechanism.

MARCUS: [reflective] The prepared mind sees connections others miss. And here's the key: Craven wasn't a researcher. He was a practitioner.

ELENA: [analytical] Which is important because practitioners see volume. They see patterns across hundreds of patients that a researcher might miss in a controlled trial.

MARCUS: [intrigued] Right. And this pattern shows up in other repurposing stories.

ELENA: [curious] Like what?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Sildenafil—Viagra. Pfizer was testing it for angina. Male patients started reporting erectile side effects.

ELENA: [amused] And instead of dismissing it as an annoyance--

MARCUS: [excited] They investigated. Created a $2 billion drug for erectile dysfunction.

ELENA: [impressed] Same pattern. Observant clinicians noticing unexpected effects.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Minoxidil for hair loss—originally a blood pressure medication. Thalidomide repurposed for cancer after the birth defect tragedy revealed its anti-angiogenic properties.

ELENA: [analytical] So the lesson is: pay attention to side effects. They're not just problems to solve—they're mechanistic clues.

MARCUS: [reflective] And create systems to capture these observations. Most clinicians notice anomalies and never report them.

ELENA: [excited] What would a systematic approach look like?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Adverse event databases that flag beneficial off-target effects. Funding for investigator-initiated studies based on clinical observations. Teaching medical students to ask "Why?" when seeing unexpected effects.

ELENA: [passionate] Because the next aspirin might already be happening in someone's clinic right now.

MARCUS: [serious] But without a system to capture those observations, they evaporate.

ELENA: [determined] Okay, second model. The Mechanism Validation Loop.

MARCUS: [analytical] This is the 74-year gap. Why scientific respectability took so long.

ELENA: [curious] What's the pattern?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Medical breakthroughs proceed through phases: observation, mechanistic explanation, rigorous validation, clinical adoption. Skipping the mechanism step—step two—dramatically slows progress.

ELENA: [reflective] Because medicine demands a "why" before accepting a "what."

MARCUS: [emphatic] Exactly. Craven had empirical evidence in 1948. But without a mechanism, the establishment dismissed him.

ELENA: [frustrated] For 23 years. Until Vane discovered COX-1 inhibition in 1971.

MARCUS: [serious] And once there was a mechanism, research accelerated. The Nobel Prize in 1982 made aspirin scientifically respectable.

ELENA: [analytical] So mechanism validation was the rate-limiting step.

MARCUS: [reflective] Right. And here's the paradox: scientific rigor demands mechanistic understanding, but mechanisms are often discovered *after* empirical observations, not before.

ELENA: [thoughtful] So do we wait for mechanism, or do we act on empirical evidence?

MARCUS: [contemplative] That's the tension. For low-risk interventions—aspirin was already known to be safe—maybe empirical evidence should be enough.

ELENA: [curious] But without mechanism, you can't optimize.

MARCUS: [impressed] True. Vane's discovery allowed dose optimization—75 to 100 milligrams instead of Craven's 300 to 600. It enabled rational combination therapies.

ELENA: [analytical] So mechanism isn't just about validation—it's about making the therapy better.

MARCUS: [excited] Right. And this shows up in other repurposing stories too.

ELENA: [intrigued] Examples?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Metformin. Used for diabetes for decades before the mechanism—AMPK activation—was discovered in the 1990s. Once the mechanism was clear, researchers started investigating new indications: PCOS, cancer, aging.

ELENA: [fascinated] So mechanism unlocks new applications.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Exactly. Lithium for bipolar disorder is the opposite example—used since 1949, mechanism still not fully understood 75 years later.

ELENA: [curious] Has that limited its use?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] It's limited optimization and analog development. We can't design better versions without understanding how it works.

ELENA: [reflective] So the lesson is: fund mechanistic research in parallel with clinical signals.

MARCUS: [agreeing] Don't wait 23 years. If you see empirical evidence, investigate the mechanism immediately.

ELENA: [determined] Alright, third model. The big one. The Public Goods Problem.

MARCUS: [serious] This is the most expensive market failure in pharmaceutical history.

ELENA: [passionate] Tell me the pattern.

MARCUS: [analytical] Pharmaceutical markets reward novel, patented molecules with exclusivity. Generic drugs—no matter how effective—have no patent protection, no exclusivity, razor-thin margins.

ELENA: [frustrated] So when a life-saving discovery involves a generic, market forces fail.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Nobody will fund the trials. The social benefit is astronomical, but the private ROI is negative.

ELENA: [reflective] And that's exactly what happened with aspirin.

MARCUS: [somber] Aspirin went off-patent in 1919. By the time Craven discovered cardiovascular benefits in 1948, it was a commodity selling for pennies.

ELENA: [calculating] No pharmaceutical company would fund $50 million trials for a drug that costs a penny per pill.

MARCUS: [serious] So publicly funded research—UK Medical Research Council, NIH—had to step in.

ELENA: [frustrated] Which took 37 years to accumulate sufficient evidence.

MARCUS: [reflective] And during that 37-year gap, an estimated 5 to 10 million people died of preventable heart attacks.

ELENA: [passionate] That's the cost of the market failure.

MARCUS: [analytical] Now look at the economics from society's perspective. Aspirin prevents $50 to $100 billion in healthcare costs annually. It costs maybe $1 to $3 billion to produce globally.

ELENA: [amazed] That's a 50-to-1 to 300-to-1 return on investment.

MARCUS: [emphatic] One of the most cost-effective interventions in medical history. But the private ROI? Negative.

ELENA: [thoughtful] So public funding is essential for generic repurposing.

MARCUS: [serious] But public funding is slower, smaller, and competes with other priorities.

ELENA: [curious] Does this pattern show up in other repurposing stories?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Absolutely. Colchicine for cardiovascular disease—ancient gout medication. Canadian government funded the COLCET trial in 2019, showed 23% reduction in cardiovascular events.

ELENA: [impressed] No company would have funded that.

MARCUS: [agreeing] Colchicine costs 10 cents a pill. And dexamethasone for COVID-19—the UK's RECOVERY trial, publicly funded, showed a generic steroid reduces mortality by 35%.

ELENA: [reflective] So the lesson is: governments have to fund generic repurposing because markets won't.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] And we need dedicated funding mechanisms. The NIH should have a program specifically for generic repurposing trials.

ELENA: [excited] What about incentives? Could we create "exclusivity lite" for companies that fund generic repurposing?

MARCUS: [intrigued] That's interesting. Give companies a few years of market exclusivity even without a new patent, if they fund the trials to prove a new indication.

ELENA: [analytical] You'd need regulatory buy-in. FDA priority review vouchers, maybe WHO-led trials for essential medicines repurposing.

MARCUS: [passionate] Because the current system is broken. We learned the lesson with aspirin and didn't fix the structural problem.

ELENA: [determined] Every generic repurposing still faces the same barriers.

MARCUS: [serious] Which means the next aspirin—if it's a generic—will probably face a 20- to 40-year delay too.

ELENA: [reflective] Unless we change the system.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] So let's synthesize. How do these three models interact?

ELENA: [analytical] They create a cascade of delays.

MARCUS: [excited] Walk me through it.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Step one: The Observer's Advantage. Craven discovers aspirin prevents heart attacks in 1948.

MARCUS: [continuing] Step two: But the establishment dismisses him because there's no mechanism.

ELENA: [building] Step three: The Mechanism Validation Loop. Observations ignored for 23 years until Vane provides explanation in 1971.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Step four: Now there's a mechanism, but the Public Goods Problem kicks in. No company will fund trials.

ELENA: [passionate] Step five: Publicly funded research slowly accumulates evidence over 14 years, from 1971 to 1985.

MARCUS: [concluding] Step six: FDA approval in 1985, definitive proof with ISIS-2 in 1988.

ELENA: [somber] Total delay: 37 years from discovery to approval. Forty years to definitive proof.

MARCUS: [reflective] And all three models reinforced each other. Without mechanism, no funding. Without funding, no validation. Without validation, observation dismissed.

ELENA: [frustrated] It's a vicious cycle.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] But here's the hopeful part: we know how to break the cycle.

ELENA: [curious] How?

MARCUS: [passionate] Listen to clinical observers. Fund mechanistic studies immediately. Create public funding mechanisms for generic trials. Design pragmatic, large-scale trials like ISIS-2.

ELENA: [excited] And fix the implementation gap—make sure people who need the drug actually get it.

MARCUS: [serious] Because proving a drug works is only half the battle. Getting it to patients is the other half.

ELENA: [determined] And on that measure, aspirin still fails. Only 40% global uptake despite universal proof and affordability.

MARCUS: [reflective] So the playbook isn't just about discovery—it's about the entire pipeline from observation to implementation.

ELENA: [passionate] And if we can fix that pipeline, the next aspirin won't take 40 years.

MARCUS: [hopeful] It might take 5.

ELENA: [energetic] That's worth fighting for.
