# The Grading

ELENA: [serious] Alright, time for the grading. We've developed this rubric to evaluate drug repurposing stories across four dimensions.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] And aspirin is going to be fascinating to grade because it's simultaneously one of the greatest successes and one of the most tragic failures in medical history.

ELENA: [analytical] Let's start with Dimension 1: Scientific Impact and Innovation. This is weighted at 30%.

MARCUS: [excited] This is where aspirin shines. We're talking about a Nobel Prize—John Vane in 1982 for discovering the COX-1 mechanism.

ELENA: [impressed] Not just any Nobel Prize. This work fundamentally changed how we understand inflammation and platelet function.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Aspirin created an entire therapeutic category. All the antiplatelet drugs that came after—clopidogrel, ticagrelor, prasugrel—they exist because aspirin proved the concept.

ELENA: [intrigued] And methodologically?

MARCUS: [passionate] ISIS-2 pioneered the "large simple trial" design. Seventeen thousand patients, minimal data collection, clear endpoints. That approach is now the gold standard for cardiovascular outcomes research.

ELENA: [analytical] So what's the score?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] I want to give it an A+, but there's a deduction I can't ignore. It took 74 years from synthesis to understanding the mechanism. 1897 to 1971.

ELENA: [contemplative] That's not really aspirin's fault—that's just how long the science took.

MARCUS: [reflective] True, but it's a factor in assessing scientific impact. The mechanism discovery came decades after clinical use.

ELENA: [agreeing] Fair. And aspirin isn't first-in-class conceptually—anticoagulation with warfarin already existed.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Right. Aspirin validated the antiplatelet versus anticoagulant distinction, but didn't invent the concept of preventing clots.

ELENA: [calculating] So... 3.8 out of 4?

MARCUS: [agreeing] That feels right. Strong A. Nobel Prize, paradigm shift, field creation—but with the mechanism delay as a minor deduction.

ELENA: [analytical] Weighted contribution: 3.8 times 30% equals 1.14.

MARCUS: [confirming] Got it. Dimension 2: Patient Impact and Lives Saved. This is weighted at 35%—the biggest weight.

ELENA: [excited] This is where aspirin should dominate. Twenty to 35 million cardiovascular events prevented since 1988.

MARCUS: [amazed] Over a million lives saved or strokes prevented every year currently.

ELENA: [passionate] And the absolute benefit is huge. ISIS-2 showed 25 lives saved per 1,000 acute MI patients. That's not marginal—that's transformative.

MARCUS: [serious] Plus, it addresses the leading cause of death globally. Cardiovascular disease kills more people than any other condition.

ELENA: [thoughtful] So this should be a perfect 4.0, right?

MARCUS: [somber] Except for two massive problems.

ELENA: [curious] The delay and the implementation gap.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Exactly. The 37-year delay from Craven's discovery in 1948 to FDA approval in 1985 cost an estimated 5 to 10 million lives.

ELENA: [frustrated] People who died of preventable heart attacks while medicine ignored the evidence.

MARCUS: [serious] And today—right now—only 40% of people with established cardiovascular disease globally are taking aspirin.

ELENA: [shocked] Sixty percent of eligible patients aren't getting it.

MARCUS: [passionate] And in low-income countries, only 16.6% receive aspirin. One in six.

ELENA: [angry] This is a drug that costs ten dollars a year.

MARCUS: [somber] The drug works brilliantly. The system fails catastrophically.

ELENA: [analytical] So what's the score? The potential is A+, but the reality is--

MARCUS: [contemplative] I'm at 3.5 out of 4. A-minus.

ELENA: [thoughtful] That feels right. Extraordinary impact, but haunted by what could have been and what still isn't happening.

MARCUS: [calculating] Weighted contribution: 3.5 times 35% equals 1.225.

ELENA: [agreeing] Okay, Dimension 3: Economic Viability and Access. Weighted at 20%.

MARCUS: [excited] This is where the numbers get absurd. Cost-effectiveness of less than $100 per quality-adjusted life-year.

ELENA: [laughing] Most interventions are considered cost-effective if they're under $50,000 per QALY. Aspirin is under $100.

MARCUS: [amused] Three orders of magnitude better.

ELENA: [calculating] And the ROI—every dollar spent on aspirin saves $50 to $300 in healthcare costs. That's a 5,000% to 30,000% return.

MARCUS: [impressed] Healthcare systems should be giving this away for free on street corners.

ELENA: [analytical] And it's universally available. Hundreds of manufacturers, 50 to 120 billion pills per year, no supply constraints.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] So again, A+ on paper. But--

ELENA: [frustrated] The market failure.

MARCUS: [serious] Nobody would fund the trials. Aspirin is generic, sells for pennies, no way to recoup R&D costs.

ELENA: [emphatic] Classic public goods problem. Enormous social benefit, negative private ROI.

MARCUS: [reflective] So publicly funded research had to step in. Which took decades.

ELENA: [analytical] And even today, despite being universally affordable, 60% of eligible patients don't take it.

MARCUS: [somber] Economic accessibility doesn't equal clinical access.

ELENA: [contemplative] What's the score?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] I'm at 3.6 out of 4. A-minus. Incredible economics, undermined by systemic failures.

ELENA: [calculating] Weighted contribution: 3.6 times 20% equals 0.72.

MARCUS: [agreeing] Last dimension: Catalytic and Systemic Impact. Weighted at 15%.

ELENA: [excited] Did aspirin change medicine beyond just preventing heart attacks?

MARCUS: [passionate] Absolutely. It transformed cardiovascular medicine from reactive to proactive. Before aspirin, we treated heart attacks after they happened. Aspirin proved we could prevent them.

ELENA: [impressed] And it inspired the entire field of drug repurposing.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Metformin for PCOS, colchicine for cardiovascular disease, dexamethasone for COVID-19—all follow the aspirin playbook.

ELENA: [intrigued] The RECOVERY trial for COVID was explicitly modeled on aspirin's success.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] And ISIS-2's large simple trial design became the gold standard.

ELENA: [analytical] So that's A or A+ territory. But did aspirin change the *system*?

MARCUS: [somber] No. And that's the problem.

ELENA: [curious] What do you mean?

MARCUS: [frustrated] The FDA still has no streamlined pathway for generic drug repurposing. The NIH doesn't have dedicated funding for it.

ELENA: [passionate] So we learned the lesson with aspirin, then forgot it.

MARCUS: [reflective] Every generic repurposing still faces the same 20- to 40-year battles that aspirin did.

ELENA: [analytical] And implementation science is still neglected. We proved aspirin works, but 60% of people who need it aren't getting it.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] So transformative within cardiology, inspirational for repurposing research, but limited systemic change.

ELENA: [contemplative] Score?

MARCUS: [deciding] 3.6 out of 4. A-minus. Catalytic in science, but not in policy or delivery systems.

ELENA: [calculating] Weighted contribution: 3.6 times 15% equals 0.54.

MARCUS: [serious] Alright, let's add it up. Scientific Impact: 1.14. Patient Impact: 1.225. Economic Viability: 0.72. Catalytic Impact: 0.54.

ELENA: [calculating] Total: 3.625 out of 4.0.

MARCUS: [reflective] That's an A-minus.

ELENA: [thoughtful] A-minus. Does that feel right to you?

MARCUS: [contemplative] It does. And here's why. Aspirin is one of the greatest medical achievements in history. It saves over a million lives every year. It costs pennies. The science is brilliant.

ELENA: [serious] But the grade has to reflect not just the drug's potential, but the reality of how that potential was squandered.

MARCUS: [emphatic] The 37-year delay from Craven to approval cost 5 to 10 million lives. Only 40% of eligible patients globally receive aspirin today. We still haven't fixed the systemic problems that caused the delay.

ELENA: [passionate] Aspirin is simultaneously medicine's greatest triumph and its most damning indictment.

MARCUS: [reflective] A small-town doctor saw the truth in 1948. The medical establishment took 40 years to listen. Lawrence Craven died of a heart attack, never knowing he was right.

ELENA: [somber] And today, in low-income countries, 83% of people who need aspirin aren't getting it.

MARCUS: [serious] If the system had worked—if Craven's observations had triggered trials in the 1950s instead of the 1980s, if we'd invested in implementation science to ensure access—this would be a clear A+.

ELENA: [determined] But the grade reflects reality, not potential.

MARCUS: [emphatic] A-minus. Extraordinary impact, haunted by preventable tragedy.

ELENA: [reflective] And the tragedy isn't over. Because the next aspirin—if it exists—will probably face the same barriers.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Unless we learn the lessons and fix the system.

ELENA: [passionate] Which is why we tell these stories. Not just to celebrate success, but to understand failure.

MARCUS: [serious] So that the next Lawrence Craven doesn't die waiting for medicine to listen.

ELENA: [energetic] Alright. That's our episode. Aspirin for cardiovascular disease prevention. Final grade: A-minus.

MARCUS: [reflective] One of the greatest medical discoveries in history, delayed by institutional failure for four decades.

ELENA: [determined] If you enjoyed this episode, share it. If you're a researcher or policymaker, think about the barriers to generic repurposing in your own work.

MARCUS: [hopeful] And if you're a clinician who's noticed something unexpected in your practice—publish it. Tell someone. Because that observation might save millions of lives.

ELENA: [energetic] I'm Elena Cross.

MARCUS: And I'm Marcus Hale. Thanks for listening to The Pivot.

ELENA: [cheerful] We'll see you next time.
