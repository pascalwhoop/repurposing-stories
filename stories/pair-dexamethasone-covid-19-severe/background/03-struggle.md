# The Struggle: Corticosteroid Controversies and Early COVID-19 Setbacks

## Important Context: No Original Development "Failure"

Unlike many drug repurposing stories, dexamethasone's original development was an **unqualified success**. It became one of the most widely prescribed corticosteroids globally and remained in continuous use from 1958 to 2020. The "struggles" in this story relate instead to:

1. The controversial history of corticosteroids in viral infections
2. Initial WHO guidance against corticosteroids for COVID-19
3. Early observational data suggesting potential harm
4. The challenge of overcoming professional skepticism to mount rigorous trials

## The Corticosteroid Paradox in Viral Infections

### Mixed Historical Evidence (Pre-COVID)

#### SARS (2003) and MERS (2012-2015): Concerning Signals
Corticosteroids had been used in previous coronavirus outbreaks with worrying results[^1]:

**SARS Studies**:
- Multiple observational studies from 2003 SARS outbreak in China and Hong Kong
- High-dose corticosteroids (often methylprednisolone 500 mg-2 g/day) commonly used
- **Concerns Identified**:
  - Delayed viral clearance (prolonged PCR positivity)
  - Possible increased mortality when given early
  - Avascular necrosis (bone death) in survivors
  - Psychiatric complications
- **Confounding**: Sickest patients received corticosteroids (indication bias)
- **Conclusion**: No clear benefit; possible harm from high doses given early

**MERS Studies**:
- Retrospective analyses of MERS patients in Middle East
- Corticosteroid use associated with higher mortality in some studies[^2]
- Delayed viral clearance documented
- Again confounded by severity (sickest patients received steroids)

#### Influenza: The Harm Signal
Studies of corticosteroids in influenza pneumonia raised additional concerns[^3]:

**H1N1 (2009) Pandemic**:
- Multiple observational studies suggested increased mortality with corticosteroids
- Meta-analysis showed association with higher risk of death, secondary infections
- Possible mechanisms: delayed viral clearance, immunosuppression

**Seasonal Influenza**:
- Some studies showed increased risk of ICU admission and mortality
- Others showed no effect when adjusted for severity

### The Mechanistic Concern: Timing Matters
These experiences led to a prevailing theory[^4]:

**Early in Disease (Viral Phase)**:
- Corticosteroids suppress antiviral immunity
- Delayed viral clearance allows unchecked replication
- Higher viral loads cause more tissue damage
- **Risk > Benefit**

**Late in Disease (Hyperinflammatory Phase)**:
- Immune system damages host tissues (cytokine storm)
- Corticosteroids dampen harmful inflammation
- Virus already controlled by adaptive immunity
- **Potential Benefit > Risk**

**The Problem**: How to identify the right timing? Most prior studies gave corticosteroids early or throughout illness, potentially explaining harm signals.

## Initial WHO Guidance Against Corticosteroids (February 2020)

### The WHO Recommendation
In February 2020, as COVID-19 spread globally, WHO issued clear guidance[^5]:

**WHO Interim Guidance (February 28, 2020)**:
> "Routine corticosteroids should be avoided unless indicated for another reason."

**Rationale**:
1. Lack of evidence of benefit in viral pneumonia
2. SARS/MERS experience suggesting harm
3. Concern about delayed viral clearance
4. Well-established risks (hyperglycemia, immunosuppression, delirium)

**Exceptions Allowed**:
- Pre-existing conditions requiring corticosteroids (e.g., asthma exacerbation, adrenal insufficiency)
- Refractory septic shock
- Use within clinical trials only

### Clinical Impact of WHO Guidance
The WHO recommendation had significant influence[^6]:

- **Low Baseline Use**: In RECOVERY trial usual care arm, only 8% received dexamethasone spontaneously
- **Professional Society Alignment**: Most national guidelines echoed WHO position
- **Investigator Hesitation**: Some proposed trials faced skepticism or opposition
- **Public Messaging**: Media reported corticosteroids as potentially harmful

## Early Observational Data: Confusing Signals

### Studies Suggesting Harm
Several early observational studies from China and Europe reported concerning findings[^7]:

**Wuhan Studies (January-March 2020)**:
- Multiple retrospective cohorts showed corticosteroid use associated with higher mortality
- Delayed viral clearance in corticosteroid-treated patients
- **Critical Confounding**: Sickest patients received corticosteroids (confounding by indication)

**European Studies (March-April 2020)**:
- Variable corticosteroid use across centers (high-dose pulses to low-dose continuous)
- Inconsistent timing (early vs. late in disease course)
- Lack of standardized protocols
- Mixed results: some showing harm, others neutral

### The Indication Bias Problem
Observational studies faced a critical methodological challenge[^8]:

**Confounding by Indication**:
- Doctors gave corticosteroids to sickest patients
- Those patients had higher mortality regardless of treatment
- Statistical adjustment could not fully account for unmeasured severity
- **Apparent harm might actually be marker of disease severity**

**What Was Needed**: Randomized controlled trial to eliminate confounding

## Professional Skepticism and Equipoise

### The Debate Among Intensivists (March-April 2020)
The critical care community was divided[^9]:

**Against Corticosteroids**:
- "We tried this in SARS—it didn't work and may have caused harm"
- "WHO and CDC guidance explicitly recommend against"
- "Delayed viral clearance could prolong infectivity"
- "Side effects (hyperglycemia, delirium) complicate ICU care"
- "Better to wait for antiviral drugs or vaccines"

**For Systematic Evaluation**:
- "COVID-19 deaths are from cytokine storm, not viral load"
- "Low-dose dexamethasone is different from high-dose methylprednisolone"
- "Timing matters—we should test late administration"
- "ARDS and sepsis data suggest benefit is plausible"
- "We have nothing else—we must test available drugs"

### Ethical Equipoise: The Trial Justification
RECOVERY investigators had to establish genuine clinical uncertainty[^10]:

**Arguments for Equipoise**:
1. **Biological Plausibility**: COVID-19 ARDS resembles other ARDS where steroids may help
2. **Sepsis Evidence**: 2018 trials showed benefit of low-dose steroids in septic shock
3. **Low Risk**: Short course of moderate-dose dexamethasone has acceptable safety profile
4. **Desperate Need**: With no proven treatments, even modest benefit would be meaningful
5. **Minimal Harm Hypothesis**: If no benefit, short course unlikely to cause significant harm

**Why Dexamethasone Specifically**:
- Well-established safety profile (62 years of use)
- Long half-life (once-daily dosing, less dose-stacking risk than methylprednisolone)
- Minimal mineralocorticoid effects (less fluid retention)
- Low cost and universal availability
- Existing on WHO Essential Medicines List

## Challenges in Trial Design and Approval

### Speed vs. Rigor Tension
RECOVERY investigators faced competing pressures[^11]:

**Need for Speed**:
- Patients dying daily
- Pandemic evolving rapidly
- Risk of missing entire outbreak wave
- Public and political pressure for answers

**Need for Rigor**:
- Definitive mortality endpoint required large sample size
- Subgroup analyses (ventilated vs. oxygen-only) needed adequate power
- Regulatory approval required comprehensive safety considerations
- Ethical review needed careful justification

### Regulatory and Ethics Hurdles
Despite urgency, standard safeguards applied[^12]:

**Regulatory Review (MHRA)**:
- Protocol submitted March 13, 2020
- Concerns about lack of placebo (open-label design)
- Justification: embedding in routine care required pragmatic approach
- **Approved March 17, 2020** (4-day turnaround)

**Ethics Review (NHS Health Research Authority)**:
- Simplified consent procedures proposed (given pandemic emergency)
- Review of risk-benefit for each treatment arm
- Consideration of vulnerable populations
- **Approved March 17, 2020** (concurrent with regulatory approval)

### The Enrollment Challenge
Even with approvals, recruitment faced obstacles[^13]:

**Hospital System Strain**:
- Clinical staff overwhelmed with patient care
- ICU capacity exceeded in many hospitals
- Shortages of PPE complicating research activities
- Burnout and fatigue among potential research staff

**Participant Concerns**:
- Seriously ill patients unable to provide consent
- Surrogate decision-makers difficult to reach (visitor restrictions)
- Fear and uncertainty about experimental treatments
- Competition from other COVID trials

**RECOVERY's Solutions**:
- Simplified consent process (legal consultative model used)
- Minimal data collection (electronically from medical records)
- No additional tests or procedures beyond standard care
- Clear communication about equipoise and urgent need

## The "Usual Care" Controversy

### Background Corticosteroid Use
Even in the "usual care" arm, some corticosteroid use occurred[^14]:

- **8% of usual care patients** received dexamethasone during follow-up
- Clinicians used corticosteroids when they judged benefit likely
- Variable timing and dosing
- **Challenge**: This diluted treatment effect estimates

**Impact on Results**:
- True benefit likely larger than observed (contamination bias)
- Intention-to-treat analysis conservative
- Strengthened evidence (benefit seen despite contamination)

### Equipoise Evolution During Trial
As preliminary data accumulated, clinical equipoise shifted[^15]:

**March-April 2020**: Genuine uncertainty; many opposed to corticosteroids
**May 2020**: Growing mechanistic understanding of cytokine storm
**Early June 2020**: Data Monitoring Committee saw mortality signal
**June 8, 2020**: DMC recommended releasing dexamethasone results

**The Dilemma**: Stop trial early (smaller sample, less certainty) or continue (more deaths in control arm)? DMC determined evidence sufficient to change practice.

## Overcoming Skepticism: What Made RECOVERY Succeed

### Scientific Factors
1. **Right Drug**: Dexamethasone's long half-life and potency suited once-daily administration
2. **Right Dose**: 6 mg moderate dose avoided excess immunosuppression of high-dose regimens
3. **Right Population**: Focused on hospitalized patients with oxygen requirement
4. **Right Endpoint**: All-cause mortality—objective, unambiguous, clinically relevant
5. **Right Timing**: Disease phase when hyperinflammation dominates

### Operational Factors
1. **Simplified Design**: Pragmatic, embedded in care, minimal data collection
2. **NHS Infrastructure**: Centralized system enabled rapid multi-site rollout
3. **Leadership**: Credible investigators (Horby, Landray) with track record
4. **Regulatory Support**: UK regulators prioritized rapid review
5. **Clinician Buy-In**: Clear communication of equipoise and urgency

### Cultural Factors
1. **Public Health Emergency**: Pandemic created willingness to try new approaches
2. **Therapeutic Nihilism**: Lack of alternatives made trials more acceptable
3. **Professional Community**: UK clinical trials culture and public trust in NHS

## The Struggle in Perspective

Unlike typical drug repurposing stories where the original drug "failed," dexamethasone succeeded spectacularly in its original indication and continued success for 62 years. The "struggle" was instead:

1. **Overcoming Historical Skepticism**: SARS/MERS/influenza data created presumption against corticosteroids
2. **Contradicting WHO Guidance**: Mounting trial against official recommendations
3. **Fighting Confounding**: Observational data couldn't answer the question; only RCT could
4. **Speed Under Pressure**: Conducting rigorous science during overwhelming pandemic
5. **Clinical Equipoise**: Convincing community that genuine uncertainty existed

**The resolution came not from discovering dexamethasone worked, but from proving it with sufficient rigor to overcome professional skepticism.**
