# Chapter 2: The Failure

MARCUS: [serious] Alright, we're back. So it's 1991, and UK-92,480 is entering Phase I clinical trials at Morriston Hospital in Swansea, Wales.

ELENA: [curious] Who's running these trials?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Dr. Ian Osterloh is the clinical director. He's going to become a very important figure in this story.

ELENA: [intrigued] What were the primary objectives for Phase I?

MARCUS: [analytical] Standard stuff—safety, tolerability, preliminary efficacy signals. You're giving the drug to a small group of patients with angina, monitoring vital signs, watching for adverse effects, seeing if there's any hint that it's actually helping with chest pain.

ELENA: [curious] And?

MARCUS: [somber] Minimal angina benefit. The patients weren't reporting significant improvements in exercise tolerance, time to angina, or reduction in nitroglycerin use.

ELENA: [concerned] So the primary endpoint is looking weak.

MARCUS: [frustrated] Very weak. Blood pressure effects were modest—not the robust vasodilation they were hoping for in the coronary arteries.

ELENA: [thoughtful] But—and I'm guessing there's a but here—

MARCUS: [amused] There's definitely a but. Male participants started reporting something... unexpected.

ELENA: [delighted] Penile erections.

MARCUS: [playful] During and after trial participation, yes. Men were experiencing erections.

ELENA: [curious] And how did the clinical teams initially interpret this?

MARCUS: [contemplative] Well, the first reaction was to dismiss it. Coincidence, placebo effect, maybe unrelated to the drug. I mean, you're running a cardiovascular trial, and suddenly patients are reporting erectile function changes?

ELENA: [analytical] But it wasn't just one or two isolated reports.

MARCUS: [serious] No. The pattern was consistent across multiple cohorts, multiple trial sites. Different groups of men, independently, reporting the same thing.

ELENA: [impressed] And here's where Osterloh's team did something really important.

MARCUS: [curious] What's that?

ELENA: [passionate] They documented it professionally. They didn't brush it off. They recorded these reports in the clinical trial case report forms, treated them as real data.

MARCUS: [fascinated] That's systematic observation.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Exactly! And that decision—to document rather than dismiss—turns out to be absolutely critical.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] So they move into Phase II trials. Expanded patient population, standard angina endpoints.

ELENA: [somber] And UK-92,480 continues to disappoint on the cardiovascular front.

MARCUS: [frustrated] Exercise tolerance? No meaningful improvement. Angina frequency? No real change. They're not seeing differentiation from existing therapies.

ELENA: [curious] But the erectile reports?

MARCUS: [intrigued] The pattern persists. Actually, it strengthens. More data, more participants, same observation.

ELENA: [analytical] Okay, so from a mechanistic standpoint, why was the drug failing for angina?

MARCUS: [curious] Yeah, Elena, explain this to me. They designed it to inhibit PDE5, preserve cGMP, cause vasodilation. Why isn't it working in the coronary arteries?

ELENA: [thoughtful] Great question. It turns out that PDE5 is not the predominant cGMP-degrading enzyme in coronary vessels.

MARCUS: [surprised] Wait, what?

ELENA: [analytical] In the heart, in coronary smooth muscle specifically, PDE3 plays a much larger role in regulating cGMP levels than PDE5 does.

MARCUS: [fascinated] So they were targeting the wrong enzyme for that tissue?

ELENA: [impressed] Not the wrong enzyme in an absolute sense—PDE5 is there, it does degrade cGMP—but it's not the main player. It's like trying to control traffic by removing one lane when there are four other lanes still open.

MARCUS: [contemplative] And there are multiple pathways regulating coronary blood flow anyway.

ELENA: [affirmative] Exactly. The NO-cGMP pathway is important, but it's not the only mechanism. You've got other vasodilatory pathways, other compensatory mechanisms. Inhibiting PDE5 alone just isn't enough to produce clinically meaningful angina relief.

MARCUS: [analytical] And they were running into dose-limiting side effects before they could get to therapeutic doses?

ELENA: [serious] Right. Headaches, flushing, nasal congestion—these are all related to vasodilation in other tissues. But before you got enough drug in the system to really help with angina, patients were getting uncomfortable.

MARCUS: [frustrated] So the therapeutic window was too narrow.

ELENA: [somber] Exactly.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] And from a business perspective, Elena, this is a disaster in the making.

ELENA: [curious] How so?

MARCUS: [serious] By 1993, they've been developing this drug for seven years. Multiple clinical trials, substantial investment. And it's not working.

ELENA: [contemplative] What's the competitive landscape looking like?

MARCUS: [analytical] Crowded. You've got nitrates, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers all well-established. Some of them are going off-patent, so generics are entering the market.

ELENA: [thoughtful] Cheaper alternatives.

MARCUS: [frustrated] Exactly. There's no clear path to differentiation. If UK-92,480 had shown superior efficacy or dramatically better tolerability, maybe you could justify a premium price. But it's showing marginal benefit at best.

ELENA: [concerned] So Pfizer is looking at this and thinking...?

MARCUS: [somber] Terminate the program. Cut the losses. This is standard industry practice—most drug candidates fail, you don't throw good money after bad.

ELENA: [intrigued] But there's this one final trial, right?

MARCUS: [serious] Merthyr Tydfil. A town in South Wales.

ELENA: [curious] Tell me about Merthyr Tydfil.

MARCUS: [contemplative] It's a former coal-mining town. By the 1990s, the mines have closed, unemployment is high. It's an economically depressed area.

ELENA: [thoughtful] And they're running a cardiovascular trial there.

MARCUS: [affirmative] Dr. David Brown is involved. He later recounted that by 1993, the outlook for UK-92,480 wasn't good. Pfizer leadership was frustrated after years of investment.

ELENA: [curious] And the results from Merthyr Tydfil?

MARCUS: [somber] Cardiovascular program: failed. No meaningful angina benefit. The data was clear—this drug does not work for chest pain.

ELENA: [serious] Program termination imminent.

MARCUS: [contemplative] Absolutely. This is where most pharmaceutical stories would end. Failed drug, sunk costs, move on to the next candidate.

ELENA: [intrigued] But there's this persistent pattern of erectile reports.

MARCUS: [amused] And here's where the urban legend comes in. You've probably heard the story about participants refusing to return their pills?

ELENA: [delighted] Is that true?

MARCUS: [playful] It's embellished, but there's underlying truth. Participants clearly valued the erectile effect despite the drug's failure to help their angina.

ELENA: [fascinated] So Pfizer is facing this dilemma.

MARCUS: [analytical] Huge dilemma. On one hand, you've got seven years of investment, multiple clinical trials, a failed cardiovascular program. Standard decision: terminate, write it off.

ELENA: [curious] On the other hand?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] You've got this reproducible clinical signal—men experiencing improved erectile function. But investigating that means pivoting to an entirely different indication.

ELENA: [intrigued] With a lot of risks.

MARCUS: [serious] Enormous risks. First, social stigma. Erectile dysfunction in the early 1990s—that's still called "impotence," framed as a psychological issue, deeply stigmatizing for men.

ELENA: [contemplative] Not seen as a medical condition.

MARCUS: [affirmative] Right. So you're asking Pfizer to pivot from a serious cardiovascular indication to what many people would have considered a lifestyle or psychological issue.

ELENA: [analytical] Reputational risk for a pharmaceutical company.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Exactly. Pfizer is a serious cardiovascular company. Are they going to become known for sexual medicine? What does that do to their brand?

ELENA: [curious] And the market—was there even a proven market for an erectile dysfunction drug?

MARCUS: [contemplative] That's the big question. Would men actually seek treatment? Would doctors be willing to prescribe it? How do you even market something like this?

ELENA: [fascinated] And financially?

MARCUS: [serious] You're looking at another fifty to one hundred million dollars to develop a new indication. New clinical trial program, regulatory pathway development, marketing expenses.

ELENA: [thoughtful] All for an uncertain return.

MARCUS: [somber] Right. So you can see why the default decision would be to terminate.

ELENA: [excited] But here's where systematic clinical observation met scientific curiosity.

MARCUS: [intrigued] Tell me.

ELENA: [passionate] Ian Osterloh and his team had documented these erectile reports across multiple sites, multiple cohorts. They had real data. Not anecdotes—actual clinical trial data showing a reproducible effect.

MARCUS: [curious] And at the same time, what was happening in the scientific literature?

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Oh, this is perfect timing. In the early 1990s, researchers were revealing something critical about the biochemistry of erections.

MARCUS: [fascinated] The same NO-cGMP pathway?

ELENA: [delighted] The exact same pathway! Turns out, nitric oxide is also essential for penile erection.

MARCUS: [amazed] Wait, so the mechanism they were targeting for cardiovascular disease is also the mechanism for erectile function?

ELENA: [excited] Yes! But here's the key—and this is where we get into the elegant biology—

MARCUS: [intrigued] I'm listening.

ELENA: [mysterious] PDE5 expression is tissue-specific. And the distribution of PDE5 in different tissues is about to explain everything—both why UK-92,480 failed for angina and why it might succeed for erectile dysfunction.

MARCUS: [energetic] Okay, we have to talk about this. This is the pivot point of the entire story.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] It absolutely is.

MARCUS: [contemplative] Let's take another quick break. When we come back, we're diving into the mechanistic revelation that turned a failed cardiovascular drug into a blockbuster.

ELENA: [excited] The science gets really beautiful here. Stay with us.
