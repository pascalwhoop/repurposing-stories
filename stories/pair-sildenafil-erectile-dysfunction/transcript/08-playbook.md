# The Playbook: Mental Models

ELENA: [energetic] Alright, so we've told the story. Now let's extract the mental models. What can we learn from Viagra's repurposing success that applies to other drug candidates?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] I think there are three big patterns here.

ELENA: [curious] Hit me with the first one.

MARCUS: [passionate] "Serendipity Prepared by Systematic Observation."

ELENA: [intrigued] Explain.

MARCUS: [analytical] This wasn't an accident. It wasn't pure luck. It was systematic documentation and investigation of unexpected clinical signals.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Right! The Phase I trials in Swansea—men reporting erections—

MARCUS: [contemplative] —and Osterloh's team could have dismissed it. "Coincidence. Placebo effect. Not relevant to angina."

ELENA: [impressed] But they didn't. They documented it professionally in the case report forms.

MARCUS: [passionate] Exactly! And the pattern reproduced across multiple cohorts, multiple trial sites. Different populations, same observation.

ELENA: [analytical] So it wasn't one isolated incident. It was a reproducible signal.

MARCUS: [affirmative] Which made it credible. And here's the key—

ELENA: [curious] What?

MARCUS: [serious] Institutional support. Someone at Pfizer had to say, "Yes, we're going to investigate this unconventional indication despite the social stigma and uncertainty."

ELENA: [impressed] That's risk tolerance. That's curiosity rewarded.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Right. And this applies broadly. How many failed drugs generate unexpected observations during trials?

ELENA: [thoughtful] Probably most of them.

MARCUS: [contemplative] Most. But the default is to terminate and move on. Viagra teaches us that systematically documenting and investigating those "side effects" can reveal alternative therapeutic opportunities.

ELENA: [fascinated] Other examples?

MARCUS: [analytical] Thalidomide. Originally a sedative, withdrawn in 1962 due to birth defects. But a dermatologist observed improvement in leprosy lesions in patients who'd taken thalidomide.

ELENA: [intrigued] Unexpected benefit.

MARCUS: [affirmative] Which led to investigation of anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects. Approved for leprosy in 1998, multiple myeloma in 2006.

ELENA: [impressed] So systematic observation of an unexpected effect led to repurposing.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Exactly! Or minoxidil—developed as an antihypertensive in the '70s. Patients reported hair growth. That "side effect" became Rogaine for hair loss in 1988.

ELENA: [amused] Hair growth as the primary application, not the side effect.

MARCUS: [playful] Right. And rituximab—developed for B-cell lymphoma. Oncologists noticed autoimmune symptom improvements in cancer patients. Led to rheumatoid arthritis, vasculitis, pemphigus vulgaris indications.

ELENA: [analytical] So the pattern is: unexpected clinical observation, systematic documentation, mechanistic investigation, new indication.

MARCUS: [passionate] That's the pattern. And it requires institutional culture that rewards curiosity and tolerates exploration of tangential findings.

ELENA: [contemplative] Okay, so Mental Model 1 is about recognizing signals. What's Model 2?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] "Mechanism-First Validation."

ELENA: [excited] This is my favorite part!

MARCUS: [amused] I figured.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Successful repurposing isn't trial-and-error. It's mechanistic insight connecting a drug's molecular action to a new disease pathophysiology.

MARCUS: [curious] Walk me through how this played out with sildenafil.

ELENA: [analytical] Okay, so step one: they understood PDE5 inhibition. Competitive inhibition at the catalytic site prevents cGMP degradation.

MARCUS: [affirmative] Right.

ELENA: [thoughtful] Step two: explaining the cardiovascular failure. PDE5 is not the predominant enzyme in coronary vessels. PDE3 plays a bigger role. So inhibiting PDE5 alone doesn't produce meaningful angina relief.

MARCUS: [fascinated] Right mechanism, wrong tissue.

ELENA: [excited] Exactly! Step three: predicting ED efficacy. Early '90s research identified the NO-cGMP pathway as critical for erection. And the key discovery—

MARCUS: [intrigued] PDE5 is more than ninety percent of cGMP-degrading activity in the corpus cavernosum.

ELENA: [triumphant] Ninety percent! So tissue-specific expression predicts efficacy. If sexual stimulation triggers NO release, sildenafil will amplify the cGMP-mediated smooth muscle relaxation.

MARCUS: [impressed] And that hypothesis is testable in pilot studies.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Which they did! Dose-dependent response. Effect requires sexual stimulation. Safety consistent with cardiovascular trials.

MARCUS: [contemplative] So mechanistic insight de-risked the development program.

ELENA: [passionate] Completely! They could predict efficacy before large-scale trials. They could optimize dosing based on target engagement. They had a clear regulatory path because the mechanism was well-understood.

MARCUS: [analytical] And the tissue-specific distribution explained both failure and success with a single framework.

ELENA: [delighted] That's the elegance! Same drug, same mechanism, but where the enzyme is expressed determines the outcome.

MARCUS: [curious] Other examples of mechanism-first repurposing?

ELENA: [thoughtful] Rapamycin. Originally an immunosuppressant—mTOR inhibition prevents transplant rejection. But mTOR regulates cell growth and proliferation.

MARCUS: [intrigued] So cancer application?

ELENA: [affirmative] mTOR pathway is dysregulated in many cancers. Rapalogs approved for renal cell carcinoma. And now aging research—mTOR inhibition extends lifespan in model organisms.

MARCUS: [impressed] Same mechanism, multiple diseases where the pathway is central.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Right! Or TNF inhibitors. Infliximab for rheumatoid arthritis. But TNF-alpha drives inflammation across autoimmune diseases.

MARCUS: [analytical] So expanded to Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, psoriasis.

ELENA: [affirmative] Understanding that TNF-alpha is a common inflammatory mediator enables mechanism-based expansion.

MARCUS: [contemplative] So the pattern is: know your mechanism deeply, understand tissue-specific expression and pathway biology, predict where else it will work.

ELENA: [passionate] Exactly! Mechanism without observation misses opportunities. Observation without mechanism can't be validated efficiently. Together, they create rational, evidence-based repurposing.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Okay, Mental Model 3. This is where I get to talk about the business and cultural side.

ELENA: [amused] Go for it.

MARCUS: [serious] "Cultural Readiness and Market Timing."

ELENA: [curious] Meaning?

MARCUS: [analytical] Even mechanistically sound, clinically effective repurposing requires cultural and market conditions that enable adoption.

ELENA: [intrigued] So it's not enough for the drug to work. Society has to be ready for it.

MARCUS: [affirmative] Exactly. And Viagra benefited from several concurrent shifts.

ELENA: [fascinated] Like what?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] 1992—one year before Pfizer decides to pivot—the NIH Consensus Development Conference reclassifies "impotence" as "erectile dysfunction."

ELENA: [impressed] Terminology matters.

MARCUS: [passionate] Huge! They emphasized organic and vascular causes over psychological etiology. Medical model replaces the stigmatizing "it's all in your head" framing.

ELENA: [analytical] So the conceptual foundation for pharmaceutical intervention is being laid right as Pfizer is making the decision.

MARCUS: [affirmative] Perfect timing. And the scientific context—Furchgott, Ignarro, Murad mapping the NO pathway, Nobel Prize in 1998—

ELENA: [excited] —legitimizes erectile dysfunction as a vascular and biochemical disorder!

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Right. Not "just" psychological. And regulatory readiness—the FDA was open to quality-of-life indications.

ELENA: [curious] Not just life-saving drugs?

MARCUS: [affirmative] Right. Quality of life matters. Clear endpoints—the IIEF questionnaire. Straightforward regulatory path.

ELENA: [contemplative] And market timing?

MARCUS: [analytical] Baby boomers aging into the ED risk group. A generation willing to discuss sexual health openly. DTC advertising regulations relaxed in 1997—

ELENA: [impressed] —one year before Viagra launches!

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Perfect timing. And celebrity endorsement—Bob Dole willing to discuss ED publicly.

ELENA: [delighted] Former presidential candidate normalizing the conversation.

MARCUS: [passionate] Exactly. All of these conditions aligned to create receptive environment for Viagra.

ELENA: [intrigued] What's the counterfactual?

MARCUS: [contemplative] What if Viagra launched in 1988 instead of 1998?

ELENA: [curious] Ten years earlier.

MARCUS: [analytical] No NIH reclassification. Still called "impotence" with psychological framing. NO-cGMP pathway not yet understood. DTC advertising regulations restrictive. Cultural stigma higher.

ELENA: [thoughtful] Slower adoption?

MARCUS: [affirmative] Much slower. Maybe not even successful commercially. Cultural context determines whether a good drug can become a blockbuster.

ELENA: [fascinated] Other examples?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Statins for cardiovascular prevention. Original use: familial hypercholesterolemia, rare genetic condition.

ELENA: [curious] But they became mass-market?

MARCUS: [analytical] Because of "cholesterol awareness" campaigns in the '80s and '90s. Framingham Heart Study popularized cardiovascular risk factor concept. High cholesterol reframed as asymptomatic risk requiring treatment.

ELENA: [impressed] So cultural readiness—prevention mindset, risk factor awareness—enabled mass adoption.

MARCUS: [affirmative] Exactly. Or SSRIs for depression. Prozac launched in 1988, but it benefited from 1990s mental health reframing.

ELENA: [intrigued] "Chemical imbalance" model?

MARCUS: [affirmative] Medical model. DTC advertising. Celebrity disclosures reducing stigma. Cultural shifts enable adoption.

ELENA: [analytical] So the pattern is: assess disease framing, patient and physician willingness to engage, regulatory environment, reimbursement mechanisms, cultural and scientific shifts.

MARCUS: [passionate] Right. Clinical efficacy alone doesn't guarantee success. Timing matters.

ELENA: [contemplative] Okay, so we've got three mental models. How do they interact?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Let me synthesize.

ELENA: [curious] Go.

MARCUS: [analytical] Model 1—Serendipity Prepared by Systematic Observation—identifies the opportunity. Clinical signal: men reporting erections during angina trials. Documentation: professional recording across multiple trials. Recognition: pattern reproducible and credible.

ELENA: [affirmative] That's the signal.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Model 2—Mechanism-First Validation—explains and validates the opportunity. Mechanistic insight: PDE5 more than ninety percent in corpus cavernosum. Predicts efficacy: tissue-specific expression explains both angina failure and ED success. Pilot trials confirm the hypothesis.

ELENA: [impressed] That's the validation.

MARCUS: [passionate] Model 3—Cultural Readiness—enables successful adoption and scale. NIH reframing in 1992. DTC advertising. Physician engagement. Market timing: unmet need, no oral alternatives, aging population.

ELENA: [excited] That's the adoption.

MARCUS: [triumphant] So together: Observation identifies, Mechanism validates, Culture enables.

ELENA: [analytical] And if you're missing any one of these?

MARCUS: [serious] Without Model 1, no signal to investigate. Opportunity missed entirely.

ELENA: [thoughtful] Without Model 2?

MARCUS: [contemplative] Clinical signal exists but can't be validated efficiently. High development risk. Uncertain regulatory path.

ELENA: [curious] Without Model 3?

MARCUS: [somber] Mechanistically sound drug fails commercially. Limited adoption despite efficacy.

ELENA: [passionate] So all three must align.

MARCUS: [affirmative] All three. That's what made Viagra work.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Alright, so if we're looking forward—if teams want to replicate this success with other drugs—what should they do?

MARCUS: [analytical] From Model 1: Capture broad safety and tolerability data in trials. Don't limit data collection to pre-specified endpoints. Investigate unexpected effects systematically. Have cross-functional teams—clinicians, biologists, strategists—reviewing anomalies regularly.

ELENA: [affirmative] Don't dismiss side effects.

MARCUS: [passionate] Right. And from Model 2: Understand target expression mapping. Know where your drug target is expressed across tissues and diseases. Develop biomarker strategies to identify responder populations. Use computational repurposing tools to predict mechanism-disease matches.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Mechanism-first, not trial-and-error.

MARCUS: [contemplative] And from Model 3: Assess disease framing. Is the condition viewed as a medical issue requiring intervention? Map stakeholders—patients, physicians, payers, regulators. Look for timing windows where cultural, scientific, or regulatory shifts create favorable conditions.

ELENA: [thoughtful] So it's not just about the science. It's about the ecosystem.

MARCUS: [affirmative] The ecosystem. And that's what sildenafil teaches us. This is a discipline, not a lottery.

ELENA: [impressed] I love that framing.

MARCUS: [amused] Alright, shall we grade this?

ELENA: [excited] Let's do it. I've been waiting for this.
