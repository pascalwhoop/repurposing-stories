# Thalidomide for Multiple Myeloma: A Drug Repurposing Story

## The Story in Brief

In 1954, German pharmaceutical company Chemie Grünenthal synthesized thalidomide as a sedative that promised safe, hangover-free sleep. Within years, it became a global nightmare when prescribed to pregnant women, causing devastating birth defects in an estimated 10,000 children worldwide. The drug was withdrawn in 1961, becoming a symbol of pharmaceutical catastrophe and the failure of safety regulation. For decades, thalidomide remained in medical exile, its name synonymous with tragedy.

But in 1994, researchers at Harvard Medical School discovered that thalidomide's harmful property—blocking blood vessel growth—could become a therapeutic weapon against cancer. Dr. Robert D'Amato and Dr. Judah Folkman published evidence that thalidomide inhibited angiogenesis, the process tumors use to build blood supply. This sparked interest from Dr. Bart Barlogie at the University of Arkansas, who suspected it might starve multiple myeloma tumors. In 1999, Dr. Nikhil Singhal published landmark results in the New England Journal of Medicine: 32% of patients with advanced, treatment-resistant myeloma responded to thalidomide alone, with 10% achieving near-complete remission. For a disease where median survival was just 30 months, this was revolutionary.

The FDA approved thalidomide for multiple myeloma in 2006, making it the first new therapy for this cancer in over 30 years. Celgene Corporation, which secured exclusive distribution rights, built a "patent fortress" around the drug's safety program, generating over $26 billion in cumulative revenue from thalidomide and its derivatives by 2015.

## Why It Matters

Multiple myeloma, an incurable blood cancer affecting plasma cells in bone marrow, kills approximately 35,000 people annually worldwide. Before thalidomide, standard chemotherapy with melphalan and prednisone offered median survival of 29-37 months, with five-year survival below 30%. Thalidomide's introduction, alongside other novel agents, increased median survival to nearly 50 months by 2007 and five-year survival to over 46% by 2010—a 45% improvement in survival during 2001-2007 alone.

Thalidomide's redemption fundamentally changed oncology. It validated angiogenesis as a therapeutic target, spawning an entire class of "immunomodulatory drugs" (IMiDs)—lenalidomide (Revlimid) and pomalidomide (Pomalyst)—that together generated $6.97 billion for Celgene in 2015, representing 75% of the company's revenue. This success enabled Bristol Myers Squibb's $74 billion acquisition of Celgene in 2019.

Beyond economics, thalidomide's story reshaped drug development policy. The FDA's 1998 approval required the strictest pregnancy prevention program ever mandated: the System for Thalidomide Education and Prescribing Safety (S.T.E.P.S.), which registered 124,000 patients between 1998 and 2004. Only one confirmed pregnancy occurred in women taking thalidomide in the U.S. under this program.

## Key Facts

- **Original use and approval year**: Sedative/hypnotic marketed in 46 countries under 37 trade names from 1956-1961; withdrawn after causing 10,000+ birth defects
- **Repurposed use and approval/adoption year**: FDA approved for multiple myeloma (with dexamethasone) on May 26, 2006; previously approved for erythema nodosum leprosum in 1998
- **The pivotal discovery moment**:
  - 1994: Robert D'Amato and Judah Folkman at Harvard discovered thalidomide inhibits angiogenesis
  - 1997-1998: Bart Barlogie and team at University of Arkansas conducted first compassionate-use trial in myeloma
  - 1999: Nikhil Singhal published NEJM paper showing 32% response rate in refractory myeloma patients
- **Impact metrics**:
  - Clinical: Increased median myeloma survival from ~30 months (1990s) to ~50 months (2007); five-year survival improved from 30% to 46%
  - Financial: Celgene revenue increased from $26.2 million (1999) to $12.9 billion (2017), with thalidomide-based drugs comprising 76% of sales
  - Regulatory: Revlimid generated $8.1 billion in 2017 (63% of Celgene revenue); generic thalidomide now available, but REMS patents protected market through 2010s

## The Lesson

Thalidomide's resurrection from pharmaceutical pariah to cancer therapy demonstrates that a drug's most dangerous property can become its greatest therapeutic value when redirected to the right disease. The discovery that thalidomide's birth defects resulted from blocking blood vessel formation revealed a mechanism that could be weaponized against cancer. This insight fundamentally validated angiogenesis inhibition as a cancer treatment strategy, spawning dozens of therapies now used across multiple cancers.

The story also reveals how tragedy shapes innovation. The FDA's unprecedented safety requirements for thalidomide's return—mandatory pregnancy testing, restricted distribution, physician certification—created the modern Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) framework now applied to numerous high-risk medications. Thalidomide proved that even drugs with catastrophic histories could be safely reintroduced when coupled with rigorous safeguards.

Finally, thalidomide illustrates how serendipity, scientific insight, and desperate need converge in drug repurposing. Multiple myeloma patients, facing certain death with no new treatments in 30 years, became willing to risk a disgraced drug. Researchers like Folkman connected basic science (angiogenesis) to clinical intuition (blood cancers need blood supply). The result was not just one repurposed drug, but an entirely new therapeutic class that has extended thousands of lives and generated billions in pharmaceutical value.
