# Chapter 2: The Wilderness Years and the Angiogenesis Insight

MARCUS: [thoughtful] For three decades after 1961, thalidomide existed in a kind of pharmaceutical purgatory. Used sparingly in the developing world for leprosy, but otherwise abandoned by mainstream medicine.

ELENA: [curious] But to understand how thalidomide became a cancer treatment, we first have to understand the disease it would eventually transform. Marcus, talk to me about multiple myeloma in the 1990s.

MARCUS: [serious] Multiple myeloma is a malignant proliferation of plasma cells—the immune cells that normally produce antibodies. These malignant cells accumulate in the bone marrow and secrete abnormal proteins.

ELENA: [analytical] And clinically, what did that look like for patients?

MARCUS: [somber] The acronym doctors used was CRAB. Calcium elevation from bone destruction. Renal insufficiency—kidney failure. Anemia from crowded bone marrow. And bone lesions causing fractures and pain.

ELENA: [concerned] What was the prognosis in the 1990s?

MARCUS: [grave] Uniformly fatal. About fourteen thousand new U.S. diagnoses per year. Median age sixty-six to seventy. And here's the critical number—median survival was twenty-nine to thirty-seven months with standard treatment.

ELENA: [shocked] Less than three years from diagnosis to death.

MARCUS: [reflective] Five-year survival was 28.8 percent. Ten-year survival was under ten percent. Virtually everyone died from their disease.

ELENA: [intrigued] What was the standard treatment?

MARCUS: [analytical] Melphalan plus prednisone. A chemotherapy regimen introduced in the 1960s. Response rates of forty to sixty percent. But nobody was cured.

ELENA: [amazed] And Marcus, what struck me when I looked at the FDA approval timelines is that there were no new myeloma drugs approved between 1962 and 1999.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Thirty-seven years. An entire generation without innovation.

ELENA: [passionate] Why? Why would the pharmaceutical industry ignore a disease affecting tens of thousands of people annually?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Several reasons. First, it was perceived as a small market—under fifteen thousand annual U.S. cases. Second, the patient population was predominantly elderly with short survival. That's not commercially attractive if you're thinking about chronic revenue.

ELENA: [analytical] But there's also a scientific reason, right?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Exactly. In the 1980s and '90s, the biology of plasma cell malignancies was poorly understood. Researchers didn't know what to target. They didn't have good molecular handles on what drove the disease.

ELENA: [curious] What about for patients whose first-line therapy failed?

MARCUS: [somber] Salvage chemotherapy became increasingly toxic and increasingly ineffective. For refractory disease—after all standard options had failed—median survival was six to twelve months. And there were no approved therapies.

ELENA: [reflective] So by the late 1990s, you had tens of thousands of patients diagnosed annually, all of whom would eventually die, and no pharmaceutical innovation for nearly four decades.

MARCUS: [serious] Which is where our story intersects with one of the most controversial figures in cancer research. Judah Folkman.

ELENA: [excited] Tell me about Folkman.

MARCUS: [passionate] In 1971, Folkman published a hypothesis that tumors cannot grow beyond one to two millimeters cubed without recruiting blood vessels. He proposed that blocking angiogenesis—new blood vessel formation—could "starve" tumors.

ELENA: [intrigued] How was that received?

MARCUS: [amused] The oncology community was deeply skeptical. They called it "Folkman's folly." For decades, this was considered a fringe idea. But Folkman persisted.

ELENA: [impressed] Through the '70s, '80s, '90s—he just kept working on angiogenesis?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Systematically. His lab at Harvard Medical School and Children's Hospital Boston developed assays to test compounds for anti-angiogenic properties. The gold standard was the rabbit cornea micropocket assay.

ELENA: [curious] How does that work?

MARCUS: [analytical] You implant a growth factor—like FGF-2 or VEGF—in the rabbit cornea, which is normally avascular. Then you measure blood vessel invasion. If a compound blocks that invasion, it's anti-angiogenic.

ELENA: [fascinated] And this is where thalidomide re-enters the story.

MARCUS: [excited] In 1993 and '94, a postdoc named Robert D'Amato was screening compounds in Folkman's lab. And he decided to test thalidomide.

ELENA: [intrigued] Why thalidomide? Given its history, why would anyone voluntarily choose to work with it?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] That's the brilliant part. D'Amato wasn't ignoring the history—he was learning from it. The birth defects were caused by disrupted limb development. And limb development requires blood vessel growth.

ELENA: [amazed] So the hypothesis was that the property causing birth defects might be anti-angiogenic activity?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Exactly! D'Amato tested thalidomide in the rabbit cornea assay. And it potently blocked blood vessel formation induced by FGF-2 and VEGF.

ELENA: [excited] Dose-dependent effect?

MARCUS: [impressed] Yes. And then D'Amato did something really clever. He tested multiple thalidomide analogs—structural variants of the molecule. And here's the key finding—

ELENA: [leaning in] Tell me.

MARCUS: [triumphant] Anti-angiogenic potency correlated with teratogenicity. The analogs that caused more severe birth defects showed stronger anti-angiogenic activity. But the sedative effects did not correlate.

ELENA: [stunned] So the mechanism that made thalidomide the worst pharmaceutical disaster in history was exactly what made it potentially therapeutic.

MARCUS: [reflective] Blocking blood vessel growth in developing limbs during days twenty to fifty of pregnancy—catastrophe. Blocking blood vessel growth in tumors—potentially therapeutic.

ELENA: [analytical] When was this published?

MARCUS: [precise] April 26th, 1994, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. The title: "Thalidomide is an inhibitor of angiogenesis." D'Amato, Loughnan, Flynn, and Folkman.

ELENA: [thoughtful] How was it received?

MARCUS: [measured] The mechanistic insight was widely accepted. After thirty-three years, scientists finally understood why thalidomide caused birth defects. But therapeutic potential? That was viewed with extreme caution.

ELENA: [curious] Because of the history?

MARCUS: [serious] The stigma was powerful. Even with a rational scientific basis, the idea of giving thalidomide to patients—voluntarily administering a drug that had caused ten thousand birth defects—that was a psychological and institutional barrier.

ELENA: [intrigued] But someone made the connection to myeloma. How did that happen?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Two years later, in 1996, a group of Italian researchers published a landmark study. Angelo Vacca and colleagues measured bone marrow microvessel density in multiple myeloma patients.

ELENA: [curious] And what did they find?

MARCUS: [excited] Significantly increased vascularity in myeloma bone marrow compared to controls. And critically—microvessel density correlated with disease stage, progression, and worse prognosis.

ELENA: [fascinated] So myeloma depends on blood vessel growth.

MARCUS: [analytical] Precisely. The biology made sense. Myeloma plasma cells reside in bone marrow, which is a highly vascular organ. The malignant cells secrete VEGF and FGF-2—the exact growth factors that Folkman and D'Amato had targeted in their angiogenesis work.

ELENA: [building excitement] So by 1996, all the pieces are in place. You have Folkman's angiogenesis hypothesis. You have D'Amato's discovery that thalidomide blocks angiogenesis. And you have Vacca's data showing myeloma depends on angiogenesis.

MARCUS: [reflective] The scientific logic was elegant. If myeloma needs blood vessels, and thalidomide blocks blood vessels, then thalidomide might treat myeloma.

ELENA: [serious] But logic is one thing. Actually giving thalidomide to human beings again—that's something else entirely.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] And this is where the story gets fascinating, because there's an anecdote—possibly apocryphal, the details are unclear—about how this moved from hypothesis to patient.

ELENA: [intrigued] Tell me.

MARCUS: [narrative] According to retrospective accounts, sometime between 1994 and 1996, Folkman received a phone call. A woman whose husband was dying from multiple myeloma. She'd read about Folkman's angiogenesis work. She asked if it could help her husband.

ELENA: [emotional] What did Folkman say?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] He suggested thalidomide based on the 1994 findings. And somehow—the details aren't fully documented—he convinced the patient's oncologist to try compassionate use.

ELENA: [curious] Did it work?

MARCUS: [measured] According to recollections, approximately one-third of these very early patients responded. But we have to be careful here—exact documentation is limited. This was before ClinicalTrials.gov existed. These were off-the-books compassionate use cases.

ELENA: [analytical] But it was enough to convince someone to do a formal trial.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Enter Bart Barlogie.

ELENA: [excited] I love this part of the story. Tell me about Barlogie.

MARCUS: [passionate] Barlogie founded the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences Myeloma Center in 1989. By the mid-'90s, it was the leading U.S. center for myeloma research. He'd pioneered high-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem cell transplant for myeloma—extending survival but not curing anyone.

ELENA: [thoughtful] So he was already known for innovative, aggressive approaches.

MARCUS: [impressed] And he was facing refractory myeloma patients who had exhausted all options. He knew Folkman's work. He'd read Vacca's 1996 data. And he was willing to overcome the stigma.

ELENA: [serious] Because his patients were dying, and he had nothing else to offer.

MARCUS: [reflective] In December 1997, Barlogie initiated a compassionate-use thalidomide trial at Arkansas.

ELENA: [intrigued] What happened next changed everything.
