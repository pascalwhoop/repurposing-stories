# Chapter 4: The Pivot Point and Rapid Renaissance

ELENA: [intense] November 18th, 1999. The New England Journal of Medicine publishes Singhal's paper. The title is deliberately understated: "Antitumor Activity of Thalidomide in Refractory Multiple Myeloma."

MARCUS: [thoughtful] The conclusion is stark and careful. Quote: "Thalidomide has substantial antitumor activity in patients with advanced myeloma. Thirty-two percent of patients had a reduction of at least twenty-five percent."

ELENA: [building excitement] That single sentence—dry, clinical, published in the world's most prestigious medical journal—represented something unprecedented.

MARCUS: [emphatic] A drug withdrawn for causing ten thousand birth defects had just been shown to work against a cancer that had no new treatments in thirty-seven years.

ELENA: [fascinated] Marcus, what I want to capture for listeners is what happened on that day. November 18th, 1999. Imagine you're a myeloma oncologist reading this paper over morning coffee.

MARCUS: [reflective] You've been practicing for decades. You've watched every single one of your myeloma patients die. You have patients in your clinic right now who are refractory, who you have nothing to offer.

ELENA: [passionate] And suddenly there's data—peer-reviewed, published in NEJM—showing that one in three heavily pretreated patients responded to a drug that's been in pharmaceutical exile for thirty-eight years.

MARCUS: [excited] Within hours, the phones start ringing. Patients who'd read about the study in the New York Times. Colleagues calling to discuss the paper. The International Myeloma Foundation receiving inquiries.

ELENA: [amazed] And at Celgene—the small biotech company that had secured thalidomide rights for the leprosy indication in 1998—executives are watching the news and realizing they're sitting on something extraordinary.

MARCUS: [analytical] Let's talk about the scientific reception. There was skepticism about the study design—single-arm, no control group.

ELENA: [thoughtful] But the effect size was compelling enough that most clinicians were willing to believe it. And Marcus, here's what struck me—the media coverage wasn't just medical journals.

MARCUS: [impressed] New York Times front page. Washington Post. BBC. NPR. Headlines like "Thalidomide's Second Chance" and "From Tragedy to Treatment."

ELENA: [reflective] For thalidomide survivors and advocates, this had to be complicated. Pride in science, but discomfort with the resurrection of the drug that caused their disabilities.

MARCUS: [somber] The Thalidomide Trust in the UK would later issue a statement: "Grateful for cancer patients but the drug's history must never be forgotten."

ELENA: [serious] And it wasn't forgotten—because within weeks, something remarkable started happening.

MARCUS: [intrigued] Off-label prescribing.

ELENA: [excited] Despite lack of FDA approval for myeloma, community oncologists began prescribing thalidomide. Dying myeloma patients and their families contacted doctors requesting "that thalidomide drug from the news."

MARCUS: [analytical] Insurance companies faced a dilemma. Payers were initially skeptical—this wasn't FDA-approved for myeloma. But they were facing terminal patients with no alternatives.

ELENA: [impressed] And most approved coverage for refractory disease on compassionate grounds. The desperation of the patient population created pressure throughout the system.

MARCUS: [fascinated] But this created an enormous problem. How do you make a catastrophically teratogenic drug widely available without repeating 1961?

ELENA: [serious] This is where we have to talk about S.T.E.P.S. The System for Thalidomide Education and Prescribing Safety.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Celgene had developed this program in collaboration with the FDA for the 1998 leprosy approval. It was the strictest safety program ever required for a drug approval.

ELENA: [curious] Walk me through how it worked.

MARCUS: [analytical] Think of it as a multi-layer verification system. Every single link in the chain—manufacturer, distributor, pharmacy, prescriber, patient—had to participate or the drug couldn't move.

ELENA: [impressed] Starting with prescribers.

MARCUS: [precise] Mandatory registration and training. Prescribers had to complete a certification exam on thalidomide risks. Commit to counseling patients extensively. Report adverse events. Re-certify annually.

ELENA: [fascinated] So only educated, committed physicians could prescribe.

MARCUS: [emphatic] Then pharmacists. Specialized training and registration. Verification of prescriber authorization. Patient counseling at each dispensing. And critically—maximum twenty-eight-day supply.

ELENA: [intrigued] Why twenty-eight days?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Forces frequent re-engagement. Every month, patients had to return, be counseled again, verify compliance. You couldn't stockpile six months of pills and disappear.

ELENA: [serious] And for patients—this is where it gets strict.

MARCUS: [analytical] For women of childbearing potential: Negative pregnancy test within twenty-four hours before starting therapy. Pregnancy tests weekly for the first four weeks, then monthly. Two forms of contraception or abstinence. Written acknowledgment of understanding.

ELENA: [impressed] For men—counseling about potential sperm transmission, latex condoms required during any sexual contact with women of childbearing potential.

MARCUS: [emphatic] And for all patients: Signed informed consent. Agreement not to share medication. Agreement not to donate blood. Complete traceability.

ELENA: [amazed] This is extraordinary. Every bottle, every pill, every patient tracked in a centralized registry.

MARCUS: [reflective] And it worked. From 1998 to 2004, S.T.E.P.S. enrolled 124,000 patients in the U.S. Forty-three percent were female.

ELENA: [curious] How many pregnancies?

MARCUS: [emphatic] Seventy-two women of childbearing potential had positive pregnancy tests during the program. After investigation, most were false positives. Three confirmed pregnancies.

ELENA: [amazed] And women actually taking thalidomide?

MARCUS: [triumphant] One. One confirmed pregnancy in a woman taking thalidomide, among 124,000 patients over six years. The therapy was stopped immediately when detected. The pregnancy resulted in miscarriage—outcome ambiguous whether thalidomide-caused.

ELENA: [passionate] One pregnancy in 124,000 patients. Marcus, this demonstrated that even catastrophic teratogenicity could be managed—if every stakeholder accepted responsibility.

MARCUS: [impressed] And it became the model. In 2007, the FDA Amendments Act formalized the REMS framework—Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy. S.T.E.P.S. transitioned to THALOMID REMS.

ELENA: [analytical] How many drugs use REMS now?

MARCUS: [precise] Over seventy medications. Isotretinoin—the acne drug that causes birth defects—uses the iPLEDGE program modeled directly on S.T.E.P.S. Clozapine for severe white blood cell depletion risk. Long-acting opioids for addiction risk.

ELENA: [reflective] So thalidomide's catastrophe created regulatory infrastructure that now protects patients across dozens of drugs.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] But we need to talk about the commercial trajectory, because this is where things get complicated.

ELENA: [intrigued] Tell me about Celgene's transformation.

MARCUS: [analytical] 1999—the year of the NEJM publication—Celgene's total revenue was twenty-six point two million dollars. Small biotech. Fewer than five hundred employees.

ELENA: [amazed] What happened to that revenue over time?

MARCUS: [impressed] 2006, around one point five billion. 2017, twelve point nine billion dollars.

ELENA: [shocked] From twenty-six million to nearly thirteen billion in eighteen years?

MARCUS: [emphatic] And in 2019, Bristol Myers Squibb acquired Celgene for seventy-four billion dollars. Among the largest pharmaceutical acquisitions in history.

ELENA: [curious] But thalidomide itself wasn't the main revenue driver, was it?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] No. Thalidomide peaked at around two hundred to four hundred million annually in 2007-2008. The real story is the next-generation IMiDs.

ELENA: [fascinated] IMiDs—immunomodulatory drugs. Tell me about lenalidomide.

MARCUS: [excited] Lenalidomide—trade name Revlimid. A structural analog of thalidomide. Four-amino-glutarimide modification. And it's one hundred to one thousand times more potent at key targets.

ELENA: [impressed] Less neuropathy risk?

MARCUS: [emphatic] Significantly lower. Major advantage over thalidomide. FDA approved for myelodysplastic syndrome in 2005, multiple myeloma in 2006.

ELENA: [amazed] And the revenue?

MARCUS: [triumphant] 2017 sales: eight point one billion dollars. Sixty-three percent of Celgene's total revenue. From a single drug.

ELENA: [shocked] Eight billion dollars annually from one drug?

MARCUS: [analytical] Then pomalidomide—Pomalyst—approved 2013 for heavily pretreated myeloma. Cumulative IMiD franchise revenue from 1998 to 2019: over eighty billion dollars.

ELENA: [reflective] Those are staggering numbers. But the financial story isn't complete without talking about the pricing controversy.

MARCUS: [serious] This is where it gets uncomfortable. Revlimid launched in 2005 at about sixty thousand dollars per year. By 2017, it was over two hundred fifty thousand. By 2020, over three hundred thousand dollars annually.

ELENA: [concerned] Ten percent annual price increases?

MARCUS: [somber] Despite no major clinical improvements. The drug worked the same in 2020 as it did in 2005. But the price quintupled.

ELENA: [passionate] And Marcus, this is where the orphan drug exclusivity debate comes in. Myeloma was granted orphan disease designation—

MARCUS: [analytical] Which is intended for rare diseases. Seven-year market exclusivity. But myeloma affects tens of thousands annually. Critics argue this was misuse of the orphan designation.

ELENA: [serious] And then there's the patent fortress strategy.

MARCUS: [intrigued] Explain this.

ELENA: [analytical] The thalidomide molecule itself was off-patent by the 1970s. Celgene couldn't patent the drug substance. But they patented the REMS distribution system. Fourteen patents on S.T.E.P.S. and THALOMID REMS.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Patents on the safety program?

ELENA: [emphatic] Registry systems, training materials, patient authorization processes, distribution protocols. And here's the trap—FDA regulation required that any thalidomide product, including generics, must use the same REMS.

MARCUS: [amazed] So generic manufacturers had to either use Celgene's REMS—

ELENA: [passionate] Which meant negotiating with Celgene, who controlled it. Or challenge the patents, which was expensive and time-consuming. Either way, it blocked generic competition.

MARCUS: [serious] NPR and ProPublica documented this in a 2018 exposé titled "How a Drugmaker Gamed the System to Keep Generic Competition Away."

ELENA: [reflective] The criticism was that Celgene used safety requirements as commercial barriers rather than purely protective measures.

MARCUS: [measured] Celgene's defense was that REMS development, maintenance, and monitoring cost hundreds of millions. The patents were justified.

ELENA: [thoughtful] Congressional hearings were held. Senators questioned whether Celgene was exploiting safety for profit.

MARCUS: [contemplative] There's no easy answer here. The pricing enabled continued innovation—third-generation IMiDs, expanded indications, next-generation therapies.

ELENA: [passionate] But it also meant that the vast majority of the world's myeloma patients couldn't access these life-extending therapies because of price.

MARCUS: [somber] In middle-income countries, Revlimid is often unaffordable. Insurance and public programs don't cover it. In low-income countries, generic thalidomide is available, but—

ELENA: [concerned] REMS implementation is challenging. Resource constraints. Inadequate pregnancy prevention infrastructure.

MARCUS: [grave] And the result—Brazil and other countries continued to document new thalidomide birth defects into the 2010s. The tragedy wasn't frozen in 1961. It was ongoing.

ELENA: [reflective] Within seven years of the NEJM publication, thalidomide went from pharmaceutical pariah to breakthrough therapy, protected by the most comprehensive safety program ever created for a drug.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] But the story doesn't end with regulatory approval and commercial success. The real measure of this repurposing isn't in regulatory precedents or revenue numbers.

ELENA: [serious] It's in what happened to patients. How many lives were extended? How much did survival actually improve? And at what cost—financial, ethical, and human?

MARCUS: [emphatic] That's where we need to look next.
