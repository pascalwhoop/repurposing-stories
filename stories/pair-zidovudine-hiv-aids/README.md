# Zidovudine for HIV/AIDS: A Drug Repurposing Story

## The Story in Brief

In the darkest days of the AIDS epidemic, when a diagnosis was a death sentence and panic gripped communities across America, a forgotten cancer drug became the first weapon in humanity's fight against HIV. Zidovudine, better known as AZT, was originally synthesized in 1964 by chemist Jerome Horwitz at the Michigan Cancer Foundation to treat leukemia. When it failed to show efficacy against cancer, it was shelved and forgotten for two decades.

Then came the AIDS crisis. By 1985, over 12,000 Americans had died from AIDS, with no treatment in sight. Scientists at the National Cancer Institute, led by Samuel Broder, Hiroaki Mitsuya, and Robert Yarchoan, were desperately screening compounds that might inhibit HIV's reverse transcriptase enzyme. In February 1985, when Mitsuya tested a compound called "Compound S" from Burroughs Wellcome's library of nucleoside analogs, it showed remarkable activity against HIV. That compound was AZT.

The transformation from laboratory discovery to approved drug happened with unprecedented speed. Clinical trials began in July 1985. By 1987, a placebo-controlled trial led by Margaret Fischl showed stunning results: only 1 death in the AZT group versus 19 in the placebo group. The FDA approved AZT on March 20, 1987—just 25 months after the first laboratory demonstration of its anti-HIV activity, making it the fastest drug approval in FDA history at that time.

## Why It Matters

AZT was the first antiretroviral drug ever approved, transforming HIV/AIDS from an invariably fatal diagnosis to a manageable chronic condition. Before AZT, people with AIDS typically died within two years of diagnosis. AZT gave them hope, time, and the foundation for future combination therapies that would eventually make HIV a treatable disease.

The drug's approval marked a turning point in pharmaceutical regulation, patient activism, and drug development. ACT UP and other AIDS activists forced the FDA to accelerate approval processes, created new pathways for expanded access to experimental drugs, and demonstrated that patients could be powerful advocates in shaping their own treatment options.

## Key Facts

- **Original use**: Synthesized in 1964 by Jerome Horwitz as an anti-cancer agent for leukemia; abandoned when it proved ineffective
- **Repurposed use**: First antiretroviral drug approved for HIV/AIDS treatment (FDA approval March 20, 1987)
- **The pivotal discovery**: February 1985 - Hiroaki Mitsuya at the National Cancer Institute demonstrated that AZT inhibited HIV reverse transcriptase; clinical trials began July 1985
- **Impact metrics**:
  - 67.5% reduction in mortality in the pivotal Phase II/III trial (1 death vs. 19 deaths)
  - First drug to reduce mother-to-child HIV transmission by 67.5% (ACTG 076 trial, 1994)
  - Approved in record time: 25 months from first laboratory evidence to FDA approval
  - Reduced mother-to-child transmission from 25.5% to 8.3% when used in pregnancy
  - Foundation for HAART (highly active antiretroviral therapy) in 1996, which transformed HIV into a manageable chronic disease

## The Lesson

AZT's journey demonstrates both the promise and the complexities of drug repurposing. A molecule designed for one disease can find unexpected utility against another when the right scientific insight meets urgent medical need. The story also illustrates the power of government-funded basic research—the NCI's systematic screening and clinical trials transformed an abandoned compound into a life-saving medicine.

But AZT also reveals the challenges of repurposing: the drug's initial $10,000 annual cost sparked fierce protests from AIDS activists, raising questions about who profits from publicly-funded research. Drug resistance emerged within a year of monotherapy, requiring the development of combination therapies. And the speed of approval, while saving lives, meant that long-term toxicities only emerged after widespread use.

The AZT story changed drug development forever. It proved that patient advocacy could reshape regulatory processes, that combination therapy could overcome drug resistance, and that repurposing forgotten compounds could save lives while pharmaceutical companies developed new molecules.
