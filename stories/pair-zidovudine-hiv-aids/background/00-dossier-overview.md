# Dossier Overview: Zidovudine for HIV/AIDS

## Executive Summary

Zidovudine (AZT, azidothymidine) represents one of the most significant drug repurposing successes in pharmaceutical history. Originally synthesized in 1964 as an anti-cancer agent and abandoned when it proved ineffective, AZT was rediscovered in 1985 as the first effective treatment for HIV/AIDS. Its approval in 1987 marked the beginning of the antiretroviral era and fundamentally transformed HIV from a death sentence into a manageable chronic disease.

## Drug Profile

**Compound**: Zidovudine (3'-azido-3'-deoxythymidine)
**Original Indication**: Leukemia and other cancers caused by retroviruses
**Repurposed Indication**: HIV/AIDS treatment and prevention of mother-to-child transmission
**Drug Class**: Nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI)
**ChEMBL ID**: CHEMBL129
**Development Phase**: Phase 4 (approved and widely used)

## Timeline Overview

- **1961**: Richard E. Beltz first synthesized AZT (unpublished)
- **1964**: Jerome Horwitz synthesized and published AZT as anti-cancer compound at Michigan Cancer Foundation
- **1964-1985**: AZT shelved after failing to show efficacy against cancer in preliminary testing
- **February 1985**: Hiroaki Mitsuya at NCI demonstrated AZT inhibits HIV reverse transcriptase
- **February 20, 1985**: Samuel Broder informed Burroughs Wellcome of positive results
- **July 1985**: Phase I clinical trials began at NIH Clinical Center
- **February 1986**: Placebo-controlled Phase II/III trial initiated (282 patients)
- **September 1986**: Trial stopped early due to clear efficacy (1 death vs. 19 deaths)
- **March 20, 1987**: FDA approved AZT for AIDS treatment (fastest approval in FDA history: 25 months from lab to approval)
- **1989**: ACT UP protests forced price reduction from $10,000 to $6,400 annually
- **1994**: ACTG 076 trial showed 67.5% reduction in mother-to-child transmission
- **1996**: AZT became backbone of HAART (highly active antiretroviral therapy) combination regimens

## The Repurposing Evidence

### Original Development Context
Jerome Horwitz at Wayne State University/Michigan Cancer Foundation designed AZT to integrate into DNA in cancer cells caused by retroviruses and halt their growth. The compound was funded by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) as part of cancer research initiatives in the early 1960s.[^1][^2]

### Discovery Trigger
The trigger for repurposing was systematic, not serendipitous. NCI scientists Samuel Broder, Hiroaki Mitsuya, and Robert Yarchoan established a systematic screening program to test compounds against HIV reverse transcriptase. Burroughs Wellcome provided nucleoside analogs from their compound library, including AZT (labeled "Compound S"). In mid-February 1985, Mitsuya's test demonstrated that AZT was active against HIV in laboratory cultures.[^3][^4]

### Clinical Validation
- **Phase I (July 1985)**: NCI scientists showed AZT could improve immune function in AIDS patients
- **Phase II/III (1986)**: Margaret Fischl-led trial of 282 patients showed 19 placebo deaths vs. 1 AZT death (p<0.001), with 99.3% survival in AZT group vs. 86.1% in placebo group[^5]
- **ACTG 076 (1994)**: Demonstrated 67.5% reduction in perinatal HIV transmission (8.3% transmission with AZT vs. 25.5% with placebo)[^6]

### Regulatory Milestones
- **March 20, 1987**: FDA approval for AIDS treatment
- **1990**: FDA approval for HIV-positive asymptomatic patients with CD4 counts below 500 cells/mm³
- **1994**: FDA approval for prevention of mother-to-child transmission
- **1996**: Incorporated into first HAART protocols

## Scientific Rationale

AZT is a thymidine analog that inhibits HIV reverse transcriptase, the viral enzyme that transcribes HIV RNA into DNA for integration into host cell chromosomes. After cellular uptake, AZT is phosphorylated to AZT triphosphate (AZTTP), which competes with natural thymidine triphosphate for incorporation into viral DNA. When incorporated, it causes chain termination due to the absence of a 3'-hydroxyl group, preventing further DNA elongation.[^7][^8]

The mechanism was well-suited for HIV because:
1. HIV reverse transcriptase has higher affinity for AZT triphosphate than human DNA polymerases
2. HIV replication depends entirely on reverse transcription
3. The compound crosses the blood-brain barrier, addressing HIV neurological complications

## Impact Summary

### Clinical Impact
- First antiretroviral drug to demonstrate survival benefit in AIDS patients
- Reduced mother-to-child HIV transmission by two-thirds when used in pregnancy
- Foundation for combination therapy that transformed HIV into manageable chronic disease
- Enabled development of entire drug class (NRTIs) and treatment paradigm

### Market Impact
- Initial pricing: $10,000 per patient per year (1987)
- After ACT UP protests: $6,400 per patient per year (1989)
- Further reductions: $3,200 per patient per year (early 1990s)
- 453 clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov involving zidovudine
- Remains in use today as part of combination therapy regimens

### Societal Impact
- Catalyzed patient activism movement (ACT UP and others)
- Reformed FDA drug approval processes (accelerated approval pathway)
- Demonstrated power of publicly-funded research (NCI) in drug discovery
- Raised ethical questions about pricing of repurposed drugs developed with public funding

## Key Limitations and Challenges

### Drug Resistance
Resistant HIV mutations developed within weeks to months of monotherapy, with resistance developing in 12/16 patients with transient HIV suppression versus 5/19 with sustained suppression in combination therapy trials.[^9] This necessitated development of combination therapy approaches.

### Toxicity
- Bone marrow suppression (neutropenia, anemia) requiring dose reduction or cessation
- Mitochondrial toxicity causing myopathy, neuropathy, and lipodystrophy with long-term use
- Reduced thymidine kinase activity in lymphocytes after prolonged treatment[^10][^11]

### Monotherapy Limitations
When used alone, AZT's effectiveness waned over time due to viral resistance and potential cellular resistance mechanisms. The paradigm shifted to combination therapy in 1996 with HAART.

## Narrative Arc Considerations for Showrunner

This repurposing story contains several potential narrative elements:

1. **Classic Redemption Arc**: Failed cancer drug → Life-saving AIDS treatment
2. **Race Against Time**: 25-month approval during peak of AIDS crisis
3. **Activist-Driven Change**: ACT UP protests reshaped drug pricing and FDA processes
4. **Scientific Persistence**: Systematic screening by NCI scientists vs. serendipity
5. **Incomplete Victory**: Monotherapy resistance → combination therapy innovation
6. **Ethical Tensions**: Public funding vs. private profit, speed vs. safety

The evidence supports all these angles. The showrunner should select the narrative arc based on which story best serves the educational and emotional goals of the episode.
