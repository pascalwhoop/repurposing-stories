# The Context: The AIDS Crisis Era (1980-1987)

## The Epidemic Emerges

### First Recognized Cases (1980-1981)

The modern HIV/AIDS epidemic emerged in the United States between 1980 and 1981, though retrospective analysis suggests HIV was circulating in the mid-to-late 1970s, with an estimated 100,000 to 300,000 people already infected during that period.[^1]

**April 1980**: Ken Horne, a San Francisco resident, was reported to the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) with Kaposi's sarcoma (KS), a rare cancer. The CDC later retroactively identified him as the first patient of the AIDS epidemic in the United States. Horne died on November 30, 1981.[^2]

**December 1980**: A Brooklyn schoolteacher died of AIDS in New York City, one of the earliest documented deaths from the disease.[^2]

**May 18, 1981**: Dr. Lawrence Mass, a gay physician in New York City, wrote the first published article mentioning what would soon become known as AIDS in The New York Native, an LGBT newspaper.[^2]

**June 5, 1981**: The CDC published a report in the Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (MMWR) documenting five young, previously healthy gay men in Los Angeles who had contracted Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia (PCP), a rare lung infection typically seen only in severely immunocompromised patients.[^2][^3] This report is often cited as the official beginning of the AIDS epidemic recognition.

### The Death Toll Accelerates (1981-1987)

The disease spread with devastating speed through specific communities, primarily gay men, injection drug users, hemophiliacs receiving blood products, and recipients of blood transfusions.

**1981**: 121 AIDS cases reported to CDC
**1982**: 887 cases reported
**1983**: 2,807 cases reported
**1984**: 6,240 cases reported
**1985**: 12,932 cases reported
**1986**: 24,559 cases reported
**1987**: 41,910 cases reported[^2]

**Cumulative deaths (1981-1990)**: From 1981 through 1990, 100,777 deaths among persons with AIDS were reported to the CDC by local, state, and territorial health departments.[^4]

### A Disease Without a Name, Then Without Hope

In the early years, the syndrome was referred to by various names that reflected both medical uncertainty and social stigma:

- **GRID** (Gay-Related Immune Deficiency) - early 1982
- **"gay cancer"** or **"gay plague"** - popular media terms
- **"4H disease"** - referring to the four groups initially recognized: homosexuals, hemophiliacs, Haitians, and heroin users

**September 1982**: The CDC officially adopted the term "Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome" (AIDS) to describe the constellation of opportunistic infections and cancers affecting patients with severe immune system collapse.[^2]

**1983**: Luc Montagnier's team at the Pasteur Institute in France and Robert Gallo's team at the National Cancer Institute in the United States independently identified the virus causing AIDS (initially called LAV and HTLV-III, later unified as HIV - Human Immunodeficiency Virus).[^2]

### Medical Landscape: Standard of Care Before AZT

**No Effective Treatments**: Before 1987, there was no proven treatment to slow HIV progression or extend life for AIDS patients. Medical care consisted entirely of:

1. **Prophylaxis for opportunistic infections**:
   - Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for Pneumocystis pneumonia prevention
   - Antifungal medications for candidiasis
   - Antibiotics for various bacterial infections

2. **Treatment of opportunistic infections and cancers as they arose**:
   - Ganciclovir for cytomegalovirus (CMV) retinitis
   - Chemotherapy for Kaposi's sarcoma
   - Radiation therapy for KS lesions
   - Various experimental protocols for PCP

3. **Supportive care**: Nutritional support, pain management, and palliative care

**Prognosis**: The median survival time from AIDS diagnosis to death was approximately 12-24 months. Once a person developed AIDS-defining illnesses (CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³ or opportunistic infections), death was virtually certain within a few years.

**CD4 Count Trajectory**: Without treatment, HIV-positive individuals experienced progressive decline in CD4+ T-lymphocytes (the primary target of HIV), from normal levels (500-1,500 cells/mm³) to AIDS-defining levels (<200 cells/mm³) over 8-10 years on average, though this varied widely.[^5]

### The Societal Context

**Stigma and Discrimination**: AIDS patients faced profound social ostracization:
- Families disowned sons and brothers
- Employers fired workers with AIDS
- Landlords evicted tenants
- Funeral homes refused to handle bodies
- Healthcare workers sometimes refused to treat AIDS patients

**Media Coverage**: Early media coverage often sensationalized the disease, emphasizing its association with gay men and characterizing it as divine punishment or a consequence of immoral behavior. Mainstream media largely ignored the epidemic in its early years.

**Government Response**:

**Reagan Administration**: President Ronald Reagan did not publicly mention AIDS until 1985, four years into the epidemic, when more than 12,000 Americans had already died. The administration resisted calls for increased funding for AIDS research, prevention, and treatment.[^2]

**1985**: Rock Hudson's AIDS diagnosis and death brought unprecedented mainstream media attention to the epidemic. Hudson, a major Hollywood star and personal friend of President Reagan, died on October 2, 1985.

**Research Funding**: Despite the escalating crisis, federal funding for AIDS research remained limited:
- **1982**: $5.6 million allocated by the National Institutes of Health (NIH)
- **1985**: $109 million allocated
- **1986**: $234 million allocated
- **1987**: $417 million allocated (the year AZT was approved)[^2]

### The Scientific Race for Treatment

Multiple institutions launched efforts to find treatments:

**National Cancer Institute (NCI)**: Samuel Broder, Hiroaki Mitsuya, and Robert Yarchoan established a systematic program to screen compounds for anti-HIV activity, focusing on inhibiting the newly identified reverse transcriptase enzyme.[^6]

**Pharmaceutical Companies**: Initially hesitant due to uncertain market size and stigma, companies like Burroughs Wellcome began providing compound libraries for screening.

**Academic Centers**: Universities including UCSF, Johns Hopkins, UCLA, and Duke established clinical research programs to study AIDS and test potential therapies.

**Clinical Trials Infrastructure**: The NIH established the AIDS Clinical Trials Group (ACTG) network in 1987 to coordinate multi-site clinical studies. Earlier trials were conducted through smaller networks and individual institutions.

### Experimental Therapies Before AZT

Desperate patients and physicians tried numerous unproven treatments:

**Suramin**: An antiparasitic drug tested in early trials but found to have severe toxicity and no clear benefit.[^7]

**HPA-23 (ammonium 21-tungsten-9-antimoniate)**: Rock Hudson traveled to Paris for experimental treatment with HPA-23, which showed some promise in laboratory studies but proved ineffective in clinical use.

**Ribavirin**: An antiviral drug tested for AIDS but found to be ineffective against HIV.

**Isoprinosine (inosine pranobex)**: An immune modulator tested in multiple trials with mixed results and no clear survival benefit.

**AL-721**: A lipid mixture extracted from egg yolks, promoted in underground markets with no proven efficacy.

**Interferon**: Various interferon preparations tested, particularly for Kaposi's sarcoma, with limited success.

None of these agents demonstrated the combination of antiviral efficacy and acceptable safety that would later characterize AZT.

### The Urgency of the Moment (1985-1987)

By the time AZT entered clinical trials in July 1985, the AIDS crisis had reached fever pitch:

**Death toll**: Over 12,000 cumulative deaths by end of 1985, accelerating rapidly
**New diagnoses**: Thousands of new AIDS cases reported monthly
**Cities hardest hit**: San Francisco, New York, Los Angeles seeing healthcare systems overwhelmed
**Blood supply**: Growing concern about contaminated blood products (though screening began in 1985)
**Pediatric cases**: Increasing recognition of mother-to-child transmission and AIDS in children

This was the context into which AZT emerged: a society in crisis, a medical establishment without effective tools, and a patient population desperate for anything that might extend life. The pressure to find a treatment was enormous, creating both opportunities for rapid action and risks of cutting corners in drug development and evaluation.

### Regulatory Environment

**FDA Drug Approval**: Standard FDA approval process in the mid-1980s typically took 8-10 years from initial drug synthesis to market approval, involving extensive preclinical testing, three phases of clinical trials, and detailed safety reviews.

**Orphan Drug Act (1983)**: Provided incentives for developing treatments for rare diseases (defined as affecting fewer than 200,000 Americans). While AIDS would exceed this threshold, the act established precedents for expedited development that would influence AZT's path.

**Treatment IND (Investigational New Drug)**: The concept of allowing access to experimental drugs for seriously ill patients existed but was rarely used before the AIDS crisis. This would become a major focus of patient activism.

This was the world in 1985-1987: a terrifying epidemic, no proven treatments, accelerating deaths, and a desperate need for medical intervention. Into this crisis came AZT, a forgotten cancer drug that would become the first weapon in humanity's fight against HIV.
