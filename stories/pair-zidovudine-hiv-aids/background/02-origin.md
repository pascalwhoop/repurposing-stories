# The Origin: Zidovudine as an Anti-Cancer Drug (1961-1964)

## The Molecular Design

### Initial Synthesis

**1961**: Richard E. Beltz first synthesized 3'-azido-3'-deoxythymidine (later known as zidovudine or AZT) but did not publish his research. This early synthesis remained unknown to the broader scientific community.[^1]

**1964**: Jerome P. Horwitz, a chemist at the Barbara Ann Karmanos Cancer Institute (then called the Michigan Cancer Foundation) in Detroit, Michigan, independently synthesized AZT and published his work, making it the recognized first published synthesis of the compound.[^1][^2]

### The Creator: Jerome Horwitz

**Jerome P. Horwitz, Ph.D.** was a distinguished medicinal chemist who spent his career at Wayne State University School of Medicine and the Michigan Cancer Foundation. His research focused on developing nucleoside analogs—modified versions of the building blocks of DNA and RNA—as potential cancer treatments.[^2]

Horwitz's expertise was in designing molecules that would interfere with DNA synthesis in rapidly dividing cancer cells. He held multiple affiliations:
- Wayne State University School of Medicine, Detroit, Michigan
- Barbara Ann Karmanos Cancer Institute
- Michigan Cancer Foundation[^2]

### The Scientific Rationale

**Target Disease**: Leukemia and other cancers caused by retroviruses

**Underlying Hypothesis**: In the early 1960s, scientists had discovered that some viruses (later classified as retroviruses) could cause cancers in animals. Horwitz and his colleagues hypothesized that:

1. Retroviruses might cause certain human cancers
2. Nucleoside analogs could interfere with retroviral replication
3. By blocking viral replication, these compounds might prevent or treat virus-induced cancers

**Molecular Strategy**: Horwitz designed AZT as a **nucleoside analog**—a modified version of thymidine, one of the four nucleosides that make up DNA. The key modification was replacing the 3'-hydroxyl group (-OH) of natural thymidine with an azido group (-N₃), creating 3'-azido-3'-deoxythymidine.

The intended mechanism was:
1. Cancer cells would take up AZT, mistaking it for natural thymidine
2. Cellular enzymes would phosphorylate AZT to its active triphosphate form
3. AZT triphosphate would be incorporated into growing DNA chains in rapidly dividing cancer cells
4. Once incorporated, the azido group would prevent further DNA elongation (chain termination)
5. Unable to complete DNA synthesis, cancer cells would die[^1][^3]

### Funding and Institutional Support

The research was supported by the **National Cancer Institute (NCI)**, part of the National Institutes of Health, as part of the federal government's expanding investment in cancer research during the 1960s.[^2] This was the era of the "War on Cancer," and nucleoside analog research represented a major area of investigation for developing chemotherapy agents.

### The Compound Library

Horwitz synthesized AZT as part of a broader program creating nucleoside analogs for potential cancer therapy. His laboratory produced multiple compounds with structural modifications to natural nucleosides, testing each for potential anti-cancer activity.

The compound was cataloged and stored in chemical libraries maintained by:
1. The Michigan Cancer Foundation
2. Later, Burroughs Wellcome Company (which obtained samples of Horwitz's compounds)
3. The National Cancer Institute's compound repository

This storage in multiple compound libraries would prove critical two decades later when researchers began screening existing chemicals for anti-HIV activity.

### Chemical Properties

**Molecular Formula**: C₁₀H₁₃N₅O₄
**Molecular Weight**: 267.24 g/mol
**Chemical Name**: 3'-azido-3'-deoxythymidine (also: 3'-azido-2',3'-dideoxythymidine)
**Structural Class**: Nucleoside analog, specifically a thymidine analog
**Key Modification**: Azido group (-N₃) at the 3' position of the deoxyribose sugar

The azido group was crucial to Horwitz's design—it would prevent DNA chain elongation while potentially allowing the modified nucleoside to be incorporated into DNA, ideally selectively targeting rapidly dividing cancer cells.

### Theoretical Advantages for Cancer Treatment

Horwitz's design offered several theoretical advantages for cancer chemotherapy:

1. **Selective toxicity**: Rapidly dividing cancer cells synthesize more DNA than normal cells, potentially making them more susceptible to nucleoside analog incorporation

2. **Cell cycle specificity**: The compound would primarily affect cells in S-phase (DNA synthesis phase), a common strategy in cancer chemotherapy

3. **Retroviral targeting**: If retroviruses did cause certain cancers, a compound that terminated DNA synthesis could block viral replication

4. **Oral bioavailability**: As a nucleoside analog, AZT had potential for oral administration, unlike many cancer drugs requiring intravenous delivery

### Contemporary Context: Nucleoside Analog Research

Horwitz's work on AZT occurred during a golden age of nucleoside analog development for cancer treatment:

**1950s-1960s**: Multiple nucleoside analogs entered clinical use for cancer:
- 6-Mercaptopurine (1953) - for acute leukemia
- Fluorouracil/5-FU (1957) - for solid tumors
- Cytarabine/Ara-C (1969) - for acute myeloid leukemia
- Fludarabine - for chronic lymphocytic leukemia

These successes encouraged researchers like Horwitz to design novel nucleoside analogs with different structural modifications, hoping to find compounds with better efficacy or safety profiles.

### Publication and Dissemination

Horwitz published his synthesis of AZT in 1964 in the peer-reviewed scientific literature, describing the chemical synthesis method and basic properties of the compound.[^1] This publication ensured that:

1. The synthesis method was available to other researchers
2. The compound's existence was documented in the scientific record
3. Other laboratories could reproduce the synthesis if needed
4. Chemical suppliers could add it to their catalogs

However, the publication did not include extensive biological testing data, as the compound would soon be abandoned when preliminary cancer studies proved disappointing.

### The Team

While Jerome Horwitz is the principal investigator credited with AZT's synthesis, medicinal chemistry work of this era typically involved small teams of:
- Synthetic chemists who performed the chemical synthesis
- Analytical chemists who confirmed compound structure and purity
- Biological screening staff who performed initial anti-cancer testing
- Research assistants and technicians

Specific team member names from the 1964 synthesis are not well-documented in the historical record, as was common for laboratory work of that era.

### Intellectual Property Status

Horwitz did not patent AZT when he synthesized it in 1964. This decision—typical for academic researchers of that era—had profound consequences:

1. **Public domain**: The compound's structure and synthesis were published without patent protection, making them freely available
2. **Government funding**: Since NCI funded the research, the compound could be considered partially developed with public money
3. **Later patent controversy**: When Burroughs Wellcome patented AZT for AIDS treatment in the 1980s, critics argued they were patenting a publicly-known compound developed with federal funds[^4]

Horwitz himself later expressed surprise that AZT became valuable, having considered it a failed experiment when it showed no efficacy against cancer.[^2]

### The Vision That Failed

Horwitz and his colleagues envisioned AZT as potentially treating:
- Leukemias caused by retroviruses
- Other cancers with retroviral etiology
- Possibly other rapidly proliferating cancers through general anti-metabolite effects

This vision was grounded in the scientific understanding of the early 1960s, when:
- The role of viruses in cancer was being actively investigated
- Retroviruses had been shown to cause cancers in animals
- Nucleoside analogs were emerging as a major class of cancer chemotherapy
- The molecular biology of DNA replication was being elucidated

What Horwitz could not know in 1964:
- Human retroviruses (HTLV and HIV) would not be discovered until the 1980s
- AZT would prove ineffective against cancer
- The same properties that made it fail as cancer treatment would make it succeed against HIV
- His forgotten compound would become one of the most important drugs in medical history

The irony is profound: Horwitz designed AZT to fight cancer by blocking DNA synthesis, but it proved too toxic to cancer cells and normal cells alike. Twenty years later, researchers would discover that the same mechanism—blocking DNA synthesis—could selectively inhibit HIV reverse transcriptase, finally giving AZT its purpose.
