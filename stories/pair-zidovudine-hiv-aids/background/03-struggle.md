# The Struggle: Failure as Cancer Treatment (1964-1985)

## Initial Testing and Abandonment

### Preliminary Anti-Cancer Studies

After Jerome Horwitz synthesized AZT in 1964 at the Michigan Cancer Foundation, the compound underwent preliminary biological testing for anti-cancer activity. These initial evaluations were conducted as standard practice for newly synthesized nucleoside analogs, testing them against cancer cell lines and potentially in early animal models.

**Results**: When zidovudine proved ineffective as an anticancer drug during preliminary testing, scientists set it aside.[^1] The compound failed to demonstrate sufficient efficacy against leukemia or other cancers to warrant further development.

**Timeline**: The testing and abandonment occurred sometime between 1964 and the early 1970s. Specific dates and detailed results from these early anti-cancer studies are not well-documented in the published scientific literature, as was typical for failed drug candidates of that era.[^1][^2]

### Reasons for Failure

While detailed documentation of AZT's cancer testing failures is limited, the likely reasons for abandonment as an anti-cancer agent include:

**1. Insufficient Selectivity**

Nucleoside analogs work by interfering with DNA synthesis. For a cancer drug to be effective, it must preferentially kill rapidly dividing cancer cells while sparing normal cells that also divide (bone marrow, intestinal lining, hair follicles). AZT likely showed toxicity to both cancer cells and normal cells without sufficient therapeutic window—the gap between effective dose and toxic dose.

**2. Toxicity Profile**

AZT's mechanism of action—terminating DNA synthesis—affects all dividing cells. The compound likely produced:
- **Bone marrow suppression**: Reduced production of red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets
- **Gastrointestinal toxicity**: Damage to rapidly dividing cells lining the digestive tract
- **General cytotoxicity**: Cell death in multiple tissues

These toxicities would later prove problematic even for HIV treatment, but they were unacceptable for a cancer drug without commensurate anti-tumor efficacy.

**3. Competition from Better Alternatives**

By the mid-1960s, several nucleoside analogs had already proven effective for cancer treatment:
- **Cytarabine (Ara-C)**: Approved in 1969 for acute myeloid leukemia, showed superior efficacy
- **6-Mercaptopurine**: Already in use for acute leukemia since 1953
- **Fluorouracil (5-FU)**: In use since 1957 for solid tumors

AZT would have needed to demonstrate clear advantages over these established agents to justify further development. It did not.

**4. Limited Understanding of Retroviruses**

Horwitz's original hypothesis—that AZT might treat cancers caused by retroviruses—was ahead of its time. In the mid-1960s:
- Human retroviruses had not yet been discovered (HTLV-1 was discovered in 1980, HIV in 1983)
- The link between retroviruses and human cancer remained speculative
- Without a clear retroviral target, the rationale for AZT as an anti-cancer agent was weakened

### The Shelf Life (1964-1985)

**Storage in Compound Libraries**

Despite its failure as a cancer drug, AZT was not destroyed. The compound was stored in chemical libraries maintained by:

1. **Michigan Cancer Foundation**: The original synthesizing institution kept samples
2. **National Cancer Institute (NCI)**: As the funding agency, NCI maintained compound repositories
3. **Burroughs Wellcome Company**: The pharmaceutical company obtained samples of nucleoside analogs, including AZT, for their own compound library

This storage proved critical. Pharmaceutical companies and research institutions routinely maintain libraries of synthesized compounds, even failed ones, because:
- Chemical synthesis is expensive and time-consuming
- Compounds might find unexpected uses
- Starting materials for derivative synthesis
- Reference standards for analytical chemistry

**Two Decades of Obscurity**

From approximately 1964/1965 until 1985, AZT remained:
- Unpublished in terms of clinical trial data (preliminary testing results were never formally published)
- Un-patented (Horwitz had not sought intellectual property protection)
- Uncited (few if any scientific papers referenced the compound)
- Unknown to most of the scientific community outside nucleoside chemistry specialists

Jerome Horwitz moved on to other research projects, synthesizing other nucleoside analogs and pursuing different approaches to cancer chemotherapy. AZT was, for him, a dead end—one of many compounds that seemed promising on paper but failed in practice.

### No Documentation of Setbacks

Unlike modern drug development, which requires extensive documentation of failures for regulatory purposes, the 1960s saw many compounds abandoned with minimal record-keeping. For AZT specifically:

**What We Don't Know**:
- Specific cancer cell lines tested
- Doses used in preliminary studies
- Animal models employed (if any)
- Detailed toxicity profiles from early testing
- Decision-making process for abandonment
- Names of researchers who conducted the testing
- Institutional review and recommendations

**Why Records Are Sparse**:
1. Failed drug candidates of the 1960s were not systematically documented
2. Preliminary negative results were rarely published
3. No regulatory requirement to maintain detailed records of abandoned compounds
4. Twenty years of storage meant records were lost, discarded, or archived without cataloging
5. Focus was on successful compounds, not failures

This lack of documentation is actually typical for failed drugs of that era. Only successful drugs or those that reached human trials generated substantial paper trails.

### The Paradox of Failure

In retrospect, AZT's failure as a cancer drug was fortunate for its later success against HIV:

**Why It Failed Against Cancer**:
- Insufficient selectivity between cancer cells and normal cells
- Toxicity to bone marrow and other rapidly dividing tissues
- No significant advantage over existing cancer drugs
- Therapeutic window too narrow

**Why These Same Properties Enabled Success Against HIV**:
- HIV reverse transcriptase is more susceptible to AZT than human DNA polymerases, providing selectivity
- Toxicity was acceptable when the alternative was certain death from AIDS
- No competing HIV drugs existed in 1985—AZT had no benchmarks to beat
- The therapeutic window, though narrow, was sufficient when carefully managed

### What Would Have Happened Without Repurposing

If AZT had not been rediscovered for HIV treatment in 1985, it would likely have remained:

1. **A footnote in chemical libraries**: One of thousands of synthesized-and-abandoned compounds
2. **Uncited in scientific literature**: The 1964 publication describing its synthesis would be a rarely-accessed historical curiosity
3. **Unknown to medical history**: Jerome Horwitz would be remembered, if at all, for other contributions to nucleoside chemistry
4. **Eventually discarded**: After decades in storage, samples might be disposed of during lab moves or reorganizations

### The Unintended Consequence of Public Funding

The fact that NCI funded Horwitz's work, and that the compound remained in NCI's repository, created a direct path from academic chemistry to AIDS treatment two decades later:

**1964**: NCI funds Horwitz → AZT synthesized
**1964-1985**: NCI maintains compound in repository
**1985**: NCI scientists (Broder, Mitsuya, Yarchoan) screen NCI compounds → AZT rediscovered

This is a textbook example of how publicly-funded basic research can yield unexpected benefits years or decades later. The investment in failed cancer drugs of the 1960s created the foundation for AIDS treatment in the 1980s.

### No Documented "Struggle" in the Traditional Sense

Unlike some drug repurposing stories where researchers fight to continue development despite setbacks, AZT's story contains no such struggle:

- **No advocacy for continued testing**: Horwitz and colleagues accepted the negative results and moved on
- **No attempts to reformulate**: No efforts to modify AZT to improve its cancer efficacy
- **No publication of negative results**: The failure was not formally reported in scientific literature
- **No institutional resistance to abandonment**: The decision to shelve AZT appears to have been straightforward and uncontroversial

The compound simply failed, was stored, and was forgotten. This is the normal lifecycle of most experimental drugs.

### Historical Perspective from Jerome Horwitz

Years later, after AZT became famous as an AIDS drug, Horwitz reflected on his original work. In interviews, he expressed:

- **Surprise** that AZT found a use after failing as a cancer drug
- **Satisfaction** that his scientific work ultimately helped people, even if not in the way he intended
- **No regret** about failing to patent the compound, as academic norms of his era discouraged patenting
- **Amazement** at how the molecule he designed in the 1960s became central to AIDS treatment[^2]

Horwitz died in 2011 at age 93, having witnessed his "failed" experiment become one of the most significant drugs in modern medicine. His obituary noted: "He was more interested in saving lives than making money."[^2]

### The Lesson of AZT's Failure

AZT's failure as a cancer drug illustrates an important principle in drug development: **failure at one indication does not mean a compound lacks all therapeutic value**. The same molecular properties that doom a drug for one disease may be exactly what's needed for another.

In AZT's case:
- The DNA chain termination that was too toxic for cancer was sufficiently selective against HIV reverse transcriptase
- The bone marrow suppression that was unacceptable for cancer patients was tolerable for AIDS patients facing certain death
- The narrow therapeutic window that made it a poor cancer drug was manageable with careful dosing for HIV

This is the essence of drug repurposing: recognizing that biological activity, even problematic activity, might be therapeutic in a different disease context.
