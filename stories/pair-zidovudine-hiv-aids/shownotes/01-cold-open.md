# Cold Open: Zidovudine (AZT) for HIV/AIDS

## The Tease (3 sentences)

In 1987, Margaret Fischl's research team faced an ethical crisis: their placebo-controlled trial of a forgotten cancer drug was saving so many lives that continuing the study meant letting people die unnecessarily. The decision to stop the trial early and give everyone the active treatment would lead to the fastest drug approval in FDA history—a 25-month sprint from laboratory to pharmacy that transformed HIV from a death sentence into a manageable disease. But this drug, called AZT, had already failed once before.

---

## Rationale

This cold open establishes three critical elements:

1. **Immediate Stakes**: The ethical crisis of a trial showing such dramatic benefit that continuing becomes immoral creates immediate tension. Listeners understand lives hang in the balance.

2. **Historical Significance**: "Fastest drug approval in FDA history" and "25-month sprint" signal this isn't just another drug story—it's a transformative moment in medicine and regulation.

3. **The Contradiction**: "Already failed once before" creates narrative intrigue. How does a failed drug become a life-saver? This question propels listeners into the episode.

The opening does NOT force an artificial "failure to triumph" narrative—the evidence genuinely supports this arc. AZT definitively failed cancer trials in 1964-65 and was shelved. The February 1985 discovery and March 1987 approval represent a legitimate transformation driven by new biological understanding (HIV reverse transcriptase vs. cancer cell DNA synthesis).

---

## What This Opening Promises

The audience should expect:
- A story about speed and urgency (AIDS crisis context)
- Scientific redemption (failed drug finding new purpose)
- Ethical complexity (trial stopping, pricing controversies, access barriers)
- Transformative impact (death sentence → manageable disease)

The episode will deliver on all these promises while adding depth through resistance mechanisms, HAART evolution, and activist-driven policy change.

---

## Alternative Opening (Not Recommended)

An alternative could focus on Jerome Horwitz synthesizing AZT in 1964, but this lacks immediate urgency. The 1987 ethical crisis is more compelling because:
- Higher stakes (people dying vs. academic chemistry)
- More dramatic (trial stopped early vs. compound shelved)
- Better foreshadowing (hints at both success and complexity)

The Horwitz origin story works better as Chapter 2, providing backstory after establishing why we care about AZT in the first place.
