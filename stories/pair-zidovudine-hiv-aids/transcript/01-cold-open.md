# Cold Open

MARCUS: [serious] September 1986. A Data Safety Monitoring Board gathers to review interim results from a clinical trial. They're testing a drug called AZT—a forgotten cancer failure from 1964—against AIDS. The board members open their data packets.

ELENA: [intense] And here's what they see. Placebo group: nineteen deaths. AZT group: one death.

MARCUS: [letting it land] Nineteen versus one. After just four months of follow-up.

ELENA: [urgent] The board faced an ethical crisis. They couldn't continue the trial. Every day they waited, people in the placebo group were dying unnecessarily.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] They stopped the trial early and gave everyone the active treatment. [excited] That decision would lead to the fastest drug approval in FDA history—a twenty-five-month sprint from laboratory to pharmacy.

ELENA: [amazed] AZT transformed HIV from a death sentence into a manageable disease. It spawned an entire drug class. It reformed FDA processes. It empowered patient activists who changed pharmaceutical pricing forever.

MARCUS: [mysterious] But here's the thing... this drug had already failed once before.

ELENA: [intrigued] In 1964, Jerome Horwitz synthesized AZT to fight cancer by terminating DNA synthesis. It didn't work. Too toxic. Killed normal cells and cancer cells without discrimination. So he shelved it.

MARCUS: [fascinated] For twenty-one years, it sat in compound libraries—forgotten, cataloged, gathering dust.

ELENA: [passionate] Until three scientists at the National Cancer Institute decided to do something systematic. They weren't looking for a miracle. They were screening every nucleoside analog they could find against a viral enzyme called reverse transcriptase.

MARCUS: [excited] One of those compounds had a label that read "Compound S."

ELENA: [energetic] This is the story of how a failed drug became one of the most important medicines in history. How systematic screening beat serendipity. How incomplete victories drive innovation. And how the right question can resurrect an answer that already exists.

MARCUS: [serious] I'm Marcus Hale.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] And I'm Elena Cross. Welcome to The Pivot.
