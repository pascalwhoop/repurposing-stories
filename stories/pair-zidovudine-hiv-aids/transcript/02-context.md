# Chapter 1: The Plague Years

MARCUS: [somber] June 5, 1981. The CDC publishes a report in their Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report. Five previously healthy young gay men in Los Angeles have come down with Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia.

ELENA: [serious] Which is bizarre, because this pneumonia only shows up in people with severely compromised immune systems. Cancer patients on chemotherapy. Organ transplant recipients on immunosuppressants. Not healthy thirty-year-olds.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Dr. Michael Gottlieb at UCLA is seeing these patients. They're dying from infections that shouldn't kill them. Their immune systems have just... collapsed.

ELENA: [concerned] And it's not just Los Angeles. By July 1981, the CDC is reporting twenty-six cases of a rare cancer called Kaposi's sarcoma in young gay men in New York and California. Again—this cancer typically appears in elderly Mediterranean men, not young Americans.

MARCUS: [analytical] The numbers tell a terrifying story. 1981: one hundred twenty-one cases. 1982: six hundred cases. 1983: three thousand cases. 1984: seven thousand five hundred cases.

ELENA: [urgent] 1985: twelve thousand nine hundred thirty-two cases. And these aren't just case numbers—these are deaths. The disease is uniformly fatal within months to years of diagnosis.

MARCUS: [somber] The medical establishment has no idea what's causing it. The names they try reveal their confusion and, honestly, their prejudice.

ELENA: [frustrated] GRID—Gay-Related Immune Deficiency. "Gay cancer." The 4H disease—Haitians, homosexuals, hemophiliacs, heroin users.

MARCUS: [serious] September 1982, the CDC finally settles on a name: Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome. AIDS.

ELENA: [reflective] But naming it doesn't mean understanding it. And it certainly doesn't mean treating it.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Let me describe what standard care looked like in 1984. You get an AIDS diagnosis. Your doctor tells you your median survival is twelve to twenty-four months. Maybe less.

ELENA: [somber] There are no treatments to slow HIV progression. None. Zero. Your care consists entirely of trying to prevent and treat opportunistic infections.

MARCUS: [analytical] Your CD4 count—normally five hundred to fifteen hundred cells per microliter—drops to below two hundred. At that point, you're vulnerable to Pneumocystis pneumonia, toxoplasmosis, cryptococcal meningitis, CMV retinitis...

ELENA: [intense] You're prescribed prophylactic antibiotics. You might get experimental immune boosters. But fundamentally, you're just... waiting to die.

MARCUS: [somber] And die you will. The trajectory is inexorable. Immune function declines. Opportunistic infections appear. You cycle through hospitalizations. Weight loss, wasting syndrome, dementia from HIV in the brain.

ELENA: [passionate] Meanwhile, researchers are desperately throwing everything at the wall to see what sticks. Suramin—an antiparasitic drug. Severe toxicity, no clear benefit.

MARCUS: [serious] HPA-23—a complex compound tested in France. Rock Hudson travels to Paris in 1985 to try it. Doesn't work.

ELENA: [frustrated] Ribavirin, isoprinosine, AL-721, interferon-alpha... one after another, they fail to demonstrate survival benefit in controlled trials.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] But people are desperate. Underground buyers' clubs spring up. Patients are willing to try anything—smuggled drugs from Mexico, unproven therapies, compounds with no safety data.

ELENA: [angry] And where is the government? Reagan doesn't publicly mention AIDS until September 1985. Four years into the epidemic. Twelve thousand people are dead by then.

MARCUS: [analytical] The funding tells the story. NIH AIDS research budget: 1982, five point six million. 1985, a hundred nine million. 1986, two hundred thirty-four million.

ELENA: [skeptical] Which sounds like a lot until you realize we're talking about an epidemic that's killed thousands and is growing exponentially. The resources are laughably inadequate.

MARCUS: [serious] And the stigma is suffocating. Families disown their sons. Employers fire people when they find out. Landlords evict. Some funeral homes refuse to handle bodies.

ELENA: [passionate] Healthcare workers—some of them—refuse to treat AIDS patients. There are documented cases of doctors and nurses declining care out of fear or prejudice.

MARCUS: [somber] October 2, 1985. Rock Hudson dies. He's the first major public figure to die of AIDS, and suddenly mainstream America pays attention.

ELENA: [reflective] His death does something. It makes AIDS real for people who'd been able to ignore it as "a gay disease" or "someone else's problem."

MARCUS: [thoughtful] By late 1985, the scientific picture is getting clearer. The virus has been identified—HIV, isolated independently by Luc Montagnier's team in France and Robert Gallo's team at the NIH.

ELENA: [intrigued] They understand the basics of how it works. HIV infects CD4-positive T cells—the immune system's coordinators. It uses an enzyme called reverse transcriptase to convert its RNA genome into DNA, then integrates into the host chromosome.

MARCUS: [analytical] The infected cell becomes a virus factory. Eventually it dies or is killed by the immune system. But HIV is smart—it mutates rapidly, outpaces the immune response, and keeps destroying more CD4 cells.

ELENA: [serious] The immune system can't keep up. Your CD4 count plummets. And once you hit that threshold—below two hundred cells per microliter—you're defenseless against infections your body would normally handle easily.

MARCUS: [somber] Certain death within years. That's the prognosis.

ELENA: [passionate] But at the National Cancer Institute, a small team is doing something different. They're not waiting for serendipity. They're not hoping for a miracle. They're being systematic.

MARCUS: [excited] Dr. Samuel Broder, Director of NCI's Division of Cancer Treatment. Dr. Hiroaki Mitsuya, a research scientist. Dr. Robert Yarchoan, a physician-scientist.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] These three have expertise in retroviruses from cancer research. They know HIV has a vulnerability: reverse transcriptase.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Human cells don't have reverse transcriptase. Only retroviruses do. If you can inhibit that enzyme selectively, you can block HIV replication without killing the host cell.

ELENA: [intrigued] In theory.

MARCUS: [serious] In theory. But they need compounds to test. So Broder starts calling pharmaceutical companies.

ELENA: [curious] And this is 1984, early 1985. The AIDS crisis is intensifying. Thousands are dying every year with no effective treatment. The urgency is extraordinary.

MARCUS: [reflective] Broder is asking these companies: Do you have nucleoside analogs we can screen against HIV? These are modified versions of DNA building blocks—compounds that might fool reverse transcriptase.

ELENA: [intrigued] Why nucleoside analogs specifically?

MARCUS: [analytical] Because reverse transcriptase uses nucleosides—adenosine, guanosine, cytidine, thymidine—to build DNA. If you give it a modified nucleoside that looks close enough to the real thing, the enzyme might incorporate it. But if that modified nucleoside can't support further chain extension, you've terminated DNA synthesis.

ELENA: [fascinated] Chain termination. The virus can't complete its lifecycle.

MARCUS: [excited] Exactly. And here's the beautiful part—pharmaceutical companies have been synthesizing nucleoside analogs for decades as cancer drugs. There are compound libraries full of them.

ELENA: [curious] So these compounds already exist. They've been tested, at least preliminarily, for safety and mechanism. That means you can move faster than de novo drug design.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Right. You're not starting from scratch. You're repurposing.

ELENA: [reflective] Burroughs Wellcome agrees to collaborate. A chemist named Janet Rideout goes through their nucleoside analog library and selects compounds to send to the NCI for testing.

MARCUS: [mysterious] Among those compounds: one labeled "Compound S."

ELENA: [intrigued] Which had been sitting in that library since 1964.

MARCUS: [somber] For twenty-one years, while thousands died of diseases that didn't yet have names, the answer was already there—waiting in a compound library for someone to ask the right question.

ELENA: [reflective] But the answer wasn't obvious. It wasn't labeled "cure for AIDS." It was labeled "failed cancer drug." And if you looked at its history, you'd understand why no one was excited about it.

MARCUS: [intrigued] That's the paradox. The same properties that made AZT useless for cancer would make it perfect for HIV. But you couldn't know that until you understood both diseases at a molecular level.

ELENA: [passionate] So before we get to that phone call in February 1985 that changes everything, we need to go back. Back to 1964. Back to a chemist named Jerome Horwitz who thought he was designing a cancer drug.

MARCUS: [contemplative] Because understanding why AZT failed for cancer is the key to understanding why it succeeded for HIV.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Let's talk about the forgotten failure.
