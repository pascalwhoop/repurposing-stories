# Chapter 2: The Forgotten Failure

ELENA: [curious] 1964. Jerome Horwitz is a medicinal chemist at the Michigan Cancer Foundation in Detroit, affiliated with Wayne State University. His specialty: nucleoside analogs.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] And nucleoside analogs are having their moment in the 1960s. This is the golden age of cancer chemotherapy based on modified DNA building blocks.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] 6-mercaptopurine, approved in 1953 for acute leukemia. Fluorouracil in 1957. Cytarabine in 1969. These drugs are saving lives by disrupting DNA synthesis in cancer cells.

MARCUS: [analytical] The strategy makes sense. Cancer cells divide rapidly. They synthesize DNA constantly. If you can interrupt that process with a fake nucleoside that gets incorporated but can't support continued chain growth, you kill the cancer cell.

ELENA: [intrigued] And Horwitz's particular interest is thymidine analogs. Thymidine is one of the four nucleosides that make up DNA. He's systematically modifying its structure to see what happens.

MARCUS: [excited] So he designs 3'-azido-3'-deoxythymidine. Let me break that down. Natural thymidine has a hydroxyl group—an OH group—at the 3' position of the sugar ring.

ELENA: [curious] That hydroxyl group is critical, right? It's where the next nucleotide attaches during DNA synthesis.

MARCUS: [impressed] Exactly. Horwitz replaces that 3'-hydroxyl with an azido group—three nitrogen atoms bonded together. N₃.

ELENA: [fascinated] So when this modified thymidine gets incorporated into a growing DNA chain, there's no hydroxyl group for the next nucleotide to attach to.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Chain termination. DNA synthesis stops dead. The cell can't replicate. In theory, the cancer cell dies.

ELENA: [thoughtful] What was his hypothesis? Why specifically target cancer with a thymidine analog?

MARCUS: [reflective] This is the early 1960s. Scientists are discovering that some viruses—retroviruses—cause cancers in animals. There's a hypothesis that retroviruses might cause human cancers too.

ELENA: [intrigued] And retroviruses depend on DNA synthesis from an RNA template. So a compound that terminates DNA synthesis could theoretically block retroviral replication and prevent virus-induced cancer.

MARCUS: [impressed] Exactly. Horwitz is funded by the National Cancer Institute as part of the War on Cancer research initiatives. He's trying to find compounds that will selectively kill cancer cells, ideally by blocking viral replication if the cancer is retrovirus-driven.

ELENA: [curious] So he synthesizes AZT. What happens next?

MARCUS: [somber] It doesn't work.

ELENA: [surprised] Just... doesn't work?

MARCUS: [serious] Preliminary testing shows it's ineffective against cancer. The problems are multiple. First, insufficient selectivity between cancer cells and normal cells.

ELENA: [thoughtful] Meaning it kills both?

MARCUS: [analytical] Right. Rapidly dividing cells—whether cancerous or not—take up the compound. Bone marrow cells, intestinal lining cells, hair follicles... all get hit. The therapeutic window is too narrow.

ELENA: [concerned] So patients would experience severe bone marrow suppression, gastrointestinal toxicity, all the classic chemotherapy side effects, without sufficient anti-cancer benefit to justify it.

MARCUS: [contemplative] Exactly. And second, it just doesn't kill cancer cells effectively enough. Other nucleoside analogs work better. Why use AZT when you have cytarabine or other established drugs?

ELENA: [reflective] And here's the irony that won't become apparent for two decades: in the 1960s, human retroviruses haven't been discovered yet.

MARCUS: [intrigued] Right. HTLV-1—the first human retrovirus—won't be identified until 1980. HIV won't be isolated until 1983. Horwitz is designing a drug to fight retroviral cancers, but he's working twenty years before anyone knows human retroviruses exist.

ELENA: [amazed] So he's operating on a hypothesis that's correct—retroviruses and DNA chain termination—but applied to the wrong disease context.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Precisely. The mechanism he designed is sound. The target is wrong.

ELENA: [curious] What does Horwitz do with AZT after it fails?

MARCUS: [somber] He shelves it. No publication of the negative results—that wasn't standard practice in the 1960s for failed compounds. He moves on to other projects.

ELENA: [reflective] But he does publish the synthesis method in 1964, right?

MARCUS: [analytical] Yes. The structure and synthesis of 3'-azido-3'-deoxythymidine are in the scientific literature. Anyone can read it. Anyone can synthesize it. It's public domain chemistry.

ELENA: [thoughtful] And crucially, Horwitz doesn't patent it.

MARCUS: [serious] No. Academic researchers in the 1960s typically don't patent their compounds. The ethos is different—you publish your work, you share knowledge, you don't think about commercial applications unless you're in industry.

ELENA: [intrigued] That decision will have massive implications twenty years later.

MARCUS: [contemplative] Absolutely. But in 1964, Horwitz considers AZT a failed experiment. It joins the ranks of thousands of other compounds that looked promising on paper but didn't pan out in practice.

ELENA: [curious] So where does it go? If it's not being developed, where does AZT physically exist?

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Compound libraries. The Michigan Cancer Foundation has samples. The National Cancer Institute has samples in their repository—they funded the work, so they catalog the compounds.

ELENA: [fascinated] And Burroughs Wellcome?

MARCUS: [analytical] Burroughs Wellcome synthesizes or acquires samples of many nucleoside analogs during this period. They're building a library of compounds for potential therapeutic uses. AZT's synthesis is published, so they can make it or obtain it.

ELENA: [reflective] So this failed cancer drug from 1964 is sitting in at least three different compound libraries—Michigan, NCI, Burroughs Wellcome—gathering dust for twenty years.

MARCUS: [serious] Twenty-one years. 1964 to 1985. While thousands of people are dying of cancers, then later of a new disease called AIDS, the compound that could treat at least one of those conditions is sitting labeled and cataloged in storage.

ELENA: [thoughtful] What's fascinating to me is what we don't know. The documentation from 1964 is sparse.

MARCUS: [contemplative] Right. We don't know which specific cancer cell lines Horwitz tested. We don't know the exact doses used. We don't know what animal models were tried, if any. We don't know the detailed toxicity profiles.

ELENA: [curious] Why is the record so sparse?

MARCUS: [analytical] Because in the 1960s, negative results don't get published. There's no regulatory requirement to document failures extensively unless you're filing an IND with the FDA. And since AZT never makes it to clinical trials for cancer, there's no regulatory paper trail.

ELENA: [reflective] So basically, "it didn't work for cancer" is all that's recorded, and then everyone moves on.

MARCUS: [serious] Exactly. Which is a loss, right? If we had detailed mechanistic data on why AZT failed for cancer—exactly how it affects different cell types, what the dose-response curves look like, where the toxicity manifests—that information might have been useful later.

ELENA: [thoughtful] But that's not how science worked in the 1960s. Negative data isn't valued the way positive data is. You publish your successes, not your failures.

MARCUS: [contemplative] And here's the deeper irony. The properties that doom AZT as a cancer drug are exactly what will make it work for HIV.

ELENA: [intrigued] Walk me through that.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Okay. As a cancer drug, AZT has two fatal flaws. First, poor selectivity—it can't distinguish between cancer cells and normal dividing cells. Second, narrow therapeutic window—the dose that kills cancer is close to the dose that causes unacceptable toxicity.

ELENA: [curious] So it's too toxic and not selective enough.

MARCUS: [excited] Right. But against HIV, those properties transform. Here's why: HIV reverse transcriptase has a much higher affinity for AZT triphosphate than human DNA polymerases do.

ELENA: [fascinated] How much higher?

MARCUS: [analytical] About a hundred-fold. So at concentrations where AZT inhibits HIV reverse transcriptase strongly, human DNA synthesis is relatively spared. That selectivity didn't exist for cancer cells versus normal cells, but it does exist for HIV reverse transcriptase versus human polymerases.

ELENA: [amazed] So the same compound that was too non-selective for cancer becomes selective enough for HIV because of enzyme-level differences.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Exactly! And second, HIV lacks proofreading capability. Human DNA polymerases have exonuclease activity—they can remove incorrectly incorporated nucleotides. HIV reverse transcriptase doesn't have that. Once AZT is incorporated into viral DNA, it's stuck there. Chain terminated. Game over for that virus.

ELENA: [thoughtful] Whereas human polymerases might be able to remove incorporated AZT or at least have redundant pathways to deal with it.

MARCUS: [impressed] Right. The cell can survive. The virus can't.

ELENA: [reflective] And there's a third factor—blood-brain barrier penetration.

MARCUS: [analytical] Yes. AZT's structure allows it to cross from blood into brain tissue. For cancer, that's not particularly useful—most cancers AZT might treat aren't in the brain.

ELENA: [intrigued] But HIV infects the central nervous system. It causes dementia, neurological complications. You need a drug that can get into the brain.

MARCUS: [excited] And AZT can. Few other early antiretroviral candidates had that property. It's a massive advantage.

ELENA: [amazed] So the compound that failed as a cancer drug in 1964 is actually perfectly designed for a disease that won't be identified until 1983. The mechanism, the selectivity, the brain penetration—it's all there, just waiting for the right target.

MARCUS: [contemplative] Which raises the question: How many other "failed" drugs sitting in compound libraries are actually perfect for diseases we haven't properly characterized yet?

ELENA: [passionate] That's why compound libraries are strategic assets. You're not storing junk—you're storing answers to questions we haven't asked yet.

MARCUS: [serious] But in 1964, nobody knows this. Horwitz has synthesized a compound that will one day save hundreds of thousands of lives. He thinks it's a failure.

ELENA: [reflective] He moves on. His career continues. He works on other nucleoside analogs, other cancer research. AZT becomes a footnote in his publication record.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Fast forward twenty-one years. It's February 1985. Samuel Broder at the NCI has established a systematic screening program for anti-HIV compounds. Janet Rideout at Burroughs Wellcome is going through the nucleoside analog library.

ELENA: [intrigued] She selects compounds to send to the NCI. Among them: Compound S.

MARCUS: [excited] Hiroaki Mitsuya sets up the experiment. HIV-infected human T cells in culture. Add Compound S. Measure viral replication. Measure cell survival.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] And the results are unambiguous.

MARCUS: [passionate] February 20, 1985. Broder calls Sandra Lehrman at Burroughs Wellcome. "We have something."

ELENA: [amazed] That phone call is the moment when Jerome Horwitz's failed cancer drug becomes the first effective AIDS treatment.

MARCUS: [serious] But before we get to the clinical trials and the dramatic results, we need to understand the systematic process that led to that phone call.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Because this wasn't serendipity. This was deliberate. Let's talk about how systematic screening beat lucky accidents.
