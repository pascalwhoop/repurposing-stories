# Chapter 4: The Verdict

MARCUS: [serious] The trial is running. Two hundred eighty-two patients. Half on AZT, half on placebo. Every four hours, around the clock, patients are taking their pills or their placebo pills. Waking up in the middle of the night. Following a brutal regimen.

ELENA: [thoughtful] And the Data Safety Monitoring Board is watching. Their job is to review interim data periodically. If there's overwhelming evidence of benefit or harm before the trial completes, they can stop it early.

MARCUS: [building tension] Because there's an ethical line. If you have clear evidence that one treatment is saving lives and the other isn't, you can't ethically continue withholding the effective treatment from half your patients.

ELENA: [intense] September 1986. Average follow-up: four months. The Data Safety Monitoring Board convenes to review interim data.

MARCUS: [serious] And what they see is unambiguous.

ELENA: [passionate] Placebo group: nineteen deaths.

MARCUS: [somber] AZT group: one death.

ELENA: [amazed] Survival rates: ninety-nine point three percent in the AZT group. Eighty-six point one percent in the placebo group.

MARCUS: [analytical] Statistical significance: p less than point zero zero one. The probability that this result is due to chance is less than one in a thousand.

ELENA: [excited] This is not a marginal benefit. This is not a ten percent improvement. This is the difference between life and death.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] And it's not just mortality. Opportunistic infections: forty-five in the placebo group developed new infections. Twenty-four in the AZT group.

ELENA: [impressed] CD4 counts increase significantly in the AZT group. These patients' immune systems are recovering.

MARCUS: [serious] The Data Safety Monitoring Board faces an ethical imperative. They cannot continue this trial.

ELENA: [intense] Because every day they wait, people in the placebo group are dying unnecessarily. The evidence is overwhelming. AZT works.

MARCUS: [somber] They stop the trial early. Patients on placebo are offered crossover to AZT. Everyone gets the active treatment.

ELENA: [reflective] This is the moment. This is when a forgotten cancer drug from 1964 becomes the first effective AIDS treatment.

MARCUS: [excited] July 23, 1987. Margaret Fischl publishes the results in the New England Journal of Medicine. But here's the thing—the FDA doesn't wait for publication.

ELENA: [amazed] March 20, 1987. The FDA approves AZT for AIDS treatment. That's before the NEJM paper even comes out.

MARCUS: [analytical] Twenty-five months from Hiroaki Mitsuya's February 1985 laboratory experiment to pharmacy shelves. Fastest drug approval in FDA history at that time.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Let's put that in perspective. Normal drug development: eight to ten years from laboratory to approval. AZT: twenty-five months.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] What made that speed possible?

ELENA: [passionate] Urgency. Thousands dying every month. Median survival twelve to twenty-four months after AIDS diagnosis. No alternative treatments. The FDA recognized that normal timelines were a death sentence.

MARCUS: [analytical] But also regulatory innovation. The FDA created expedited review pathways. They accepted surrogate endpoints—CD4 counts as a marker of efficacy, not requiring years of survival data.

ELENA: [intrigued] Treatment IND—Investigational New Drug programs that allowed compassionate use before formal approval.

MARCUS: [impressed] And the agency brought in patient representatives to advisory committees. Patients weren't just subjects—they were stakeholders in the decision-making process.

ELENA: [serious] AZT's approval didn't just give patients a drug. It reformed how the FDA operates. Those regulatory innovations are still in use today for cancer drugs, rare diseases, other infectious diseases.

MARCUS: [reflective] But approval creates a new question: Who pays?

ELENA: [concerned] March 1987. Burroughs Wellcome announces the price. Ten thousand dollars per patient per year.

MARCUS: [somber] Ten thousand dollars. The most expensive drug in history at that time.

ELENA: [angry] And the outrage is immediate. This compound was synthesized in 1964 with NIH funding. Jerome Horwitz didn't patent it. The discovery that it worked against HIV came from NCI scientists—government employees.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] Burroughs Wellcome's argument is that development costs were high. Clinical trials are expensive. Regulatory submissions require millions of dollars.

ELENA: [skeptical] But they didn't invent the compound. They didn't do the initial screening. The government did most of the basic science. Burroughs Wellcome essentially patented a new use for a publicly-known molecule.

MARCUS: [analytical] And here's the thing—ten thousand dollars per year in 1987 is catastrophic for most patients. Many don't have insurance that covers AIDS treatment. The disease disproportionately affects communities with less economic power.

ELENA: [passionate] So you have this life-saving drug, proven to reduce mortality by ninety-five percent, and it's priced out of reach for many of the people who need it most.

MARCUS: [serious] This is where activism enters the story.

ELENA: [excited] March 12, 1987. ACT UP—the AIDS Coalition to Unleash Power—is founded in New York City.

MARCUS: [intrigued] Larry Kramer, a playwright and activist, gives a speech at the LGBT Community Center. Two days later, two hundred fifty people show up for ACT UP's first action at Wall Street.

ELENA: [enthusiastic] Just twelve days after the organization forms, they're protesting on Wall Street. Demanding faster FDA approvals. Lower drug prices. Greater access to experimental therapies.

MARCUS: [impressed] They're not asking politely. They're disrupting. Civil disobedience. Media-savvy actions designed to generate attention.

ELENA: [passionate] And their tactics work. But the most dramatic action comes two years later.

MARCUS: [intrigued] September 14, 1989. Seven ACT UP activists infiltrate the New York Stock Exchange.

ELENA: [excited] They chain themselves to the VIP balcony. They unfurl a banner: "SELL WELLCOME."

MARCUS: [amazed] Trading is briefly disrupted. National media covers it. The image of activists chained to the Stock Exchange balcony demanding lower drug prices becomes iconic.

ELENA: [triumphant] And days later, Burroughs Wellcome announces a price reduction. From ten thousand dollars to sixty-four hundred dollars. A thirty-six percent cut.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] That's still expensive. But it's proof that direct action works. Pharmaceutical companies will respond to public pressure.

ELENA: [analytical] Over the next few years, the price drops further. Early 1990s: thirty-two hundred dollars. Eventually, generic production drives it below five hundred dollars in developing countries, below a hundred dollars in low-income countries today.

MARCUS: [serious] But that initial pricing crisis reveals a fundamental tension. Who should profit from publicly-funded research? How do you balance incentivizing pharmaceutical companies to develop drugs with ensuring patients can access them?

ELENA: [reflective] These questions don't have easy answers. But ACT UP demonstrates that patients are not powerless. They can challenge pricing. They can demand policy changes. They can reshape the pharmaceutical industry.

MARCUS: [thoughtful] And the FDA responds. Not just to activism, but to the science. Because AZT's approval is just the beginning.

ELENA: [curious] What do you mean?

MARCUS: [analytical] 1990. The FDA expands approval to asymptomatic patients with CD4 counts below five hundred. That dramatically increases the population who can benefit—now you don't have to wait until you're symptomatic to start treatment.

ELENA: [intrigued] So earlier intervention. Trying to preserve immune function before it collapses completely.

MARCUS: [excited] And then 1994. The ACTG 076 trial shows something extraordinary.

ELENA: [curious] What's ACTG 076?

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] A trial testing whether AZT can prevent mother-to-child transmission of HIV during pregnancy and delivery.

ELENA: [amazed] And the results are stunning. Placebo group: twenty-five point five percent of babies born to HIV-positive mothers are infected. AZT group: eight point three percent.

MARCUS: [impressed] Sixty-seven point five percent reduction in perinatal transmission.

ELENA: [passionate] Think about what that means. In the United States from 1994 to 2000, perinatal HIV transmission drops from twenty-five percent to under two percent. Thousands of infant infections prevented.

MARCUS: [analytical] Globally, from 1994 to present, prevention of mother-to-child transmission programs incorporating AZT have prevented an estimated one to two million infant infections.

ELENA: [amazed] One to two million children who didn't get HIV because their mothers had access to AZT. That's an entire generation.

MARCUS: [reflective] In developed countries with good prenatal care, pediatric AIDS is virtually eliminated. It's one of the great public health triumphs of the late twentieth century.

ELENA: [thoughtful] So by the mid-1990s, AZT is approved for symptomatic AIDS, for asymptomatic patients with low CD4 counts, and for preventing mother-to-child transmission. It's become the backbone of HIV treatment.

MARCUS: [serious] But there's a problem. Patients on AZT monotherapy are starting to experience something troubling.

ELENA: [concerned] What are they seeing?

MARCUS: [somber] Viral rebound. Six to twelve months after starting AZT, HIV RNA levels start increasing again despite continued treatment. CD4 counts decline. Clinical symptoms return.

ELENA: [worried] The virus is coming back.

MARCUS: [analytical] And when researchers sequence the virus from these patients, they find mutations. Specific changes in the reverse transcriptase gene that make HIV resistant to AZT.

ELENA: [intrigued] Drug resistance.

MARCUS: [serious] Drug resistance. And it's nearly universal. Almost everyone on long-term AZT monotherapy develops resistant virus.

ELENA: [thoughtful] Which means AZT isn't a cure. It's not even a long-term solution by itself.

MARCUS: [reflective] It bought time. It proved HIV could be fought. It transformed AIDS from immediately fatal to... delayed death.

ELENA: [passionate] But "delayed death" isn't good enough. Patients didn't fight for access to AZT just to die a few years later instead of a few months later.

MARCUS: [serious] So the question becomes: If monotherapy fails, what's next?

ELENA: [excited] And the answer to that question will revolutionize HIV treatment. But it's going to take a paradigm shift—from thinking about single drugs to thinking about combinations.

MARCUS: [enthusiastic] Let's talk about the incomplete victory and how it drives innovation. Because the story of AZT doesn't end with resistance. It evolves.
